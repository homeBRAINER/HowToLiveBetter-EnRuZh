## How to read

- **To filter recommendations:** open the [search page](https://eternity4719.github.io/HowToLiveBetter/). Combine keywords, chapter, evidence grade and three cost dimensions: money, time and willpower. The page reads the text directly from book/, so changes to the text also change the search page.
- **To read in order:** recommendations in each chapter run from higher to lower benefit relative to cost. Start with the first few entries in each chapter.
- **If the numbers are unclear:** every entry has an “In plain language” line. It turns risk ratios and confidence intervals under “Benefit” into everyday wording, such as “about a fifth less likely to die over the same period” or “this many days in detention and this much in fines.” It uses the facts in the entry without introducing new numbers. Read this line to make sense of the advice; “Benefit” keeps the detailed figures and confidence intervals for you to check.
- **For the strongest conclusions:** select evidence grade A on the search page to retain 328 entries with specific figures from meta-analyses or large trials.
- **For the best return on your resources:** select “Very high” benefit relative to cost. This gives 89 entries requiring no money, time or willpower, with benefits in the largest category. Add a “What you gain” filter for a priority list within that type of benefit.
- **Do not be put off by chapter titles beginning with “Don’t” or “Avoid”:** they name the outcome the chapter aims to prevent, such as early death or wasted time. They do not make every entry a prohibition. Each recommendation’s title starts with a verb and tells you what to do or avoid. Both occur in the same chapter: chapter 4 includes “Turn an intention into a plan: when, where and under what conditions you will act” and “Don’t watch television or rolling news.” Follow the entry’s title rather than applying the chapter title’s tone to every recommendation.

Here is what a recommendation looks like:

```markdown
### 5. Replace household salt with a lower-sodium potassium-containing substitute if it is suitable for you
- Cost: About 3.20 USD for an 11 oz container in the US example or 7.75 EUR for 350 g in Germany; these are full package prices, not the extra cost over regular salt. See the price examples linked below.
- In plain language: In a randomized trial of 20,000 people, those who replaced their household salt with a lower-sodium substitute had a death rate about 12% lower and a stroke rate about 14% lower over roughly five years. Random assignment makes this evidence more convincing than ordinary observational data.
- Benefit: Stroke reduced by 14%, cardiovascular events by 13% and all-cause mortality by 12%.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Neal B, et al. (2021). NEJM. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2105675
- Notes: Disputed. Do not use if you have impaired kidney function or take potassium-sparing diuretics. An ecological study of 181 countries found that countries with higher sodium intake instead had longer life expectancy and lower all-cause mortality (β = −131 cases per gram of daily sodium intake, R² = 0.60, P < 0.001). Its authors therefore argue against treating sodium as the culprit in shorter lives. However, an ecological study compares countries, not individuals: wealthier countries consume more salt and live longer. Economic conditions cannot be ruled out as a confounder, so this evidence ranks below the randomized trial above. Source: Messerli FH, Hofstetter L, Syrogiannouli L, et al. (2021). Sodium intake, life expectancy, and all-cause mortality. European Heart Journal, 42(21), 2103-2112. <https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehaa947>
```

## Four resources

This guide aims to make better use of four resources, not just extend lifespan:

- **Lifespan:** live longer and avoid deaths that could have been prevented.
- **Time and energy:** keep unproductive activities from consuming your life, attention and physical energy.
- **Money:** waste less money and spend it where the benefit is clear.
- **Personal freedom:** avoid ending up in administrative detention or pretrial custody because you did not know a legal boundary.

Every recommendation answers two questions: what you spend (money, time, energy or willpower) and what you gain (a change in all-cause mortality, fewer deaths from a specific cause, time and energy saved, money saved, social protection or personal freedom). Entries are ordered by benefit relative to cost rather than by category: large benefits at nearly zero cost come first.

**Benefits are grouped by who receives them.** In descending order of the expected benefit returning to you: ① **yourself**; ② **your spouse and direct relatives** — parents, children, grandparents on both sides and grandchildren; ③ **friends, colleagues and other relatives** — reciprocal relationships in which help you give may come back to you; ④ **strangers** — the lowest category, but not zero: a return is less likely, you do not know their character, and you may face extortion, accusations or retaliation. Benefits across groups are not added together; entries concerning the fourth group describe both benefits and risks.

Read the first-aid chapter in the same way. Of 38,227 out-of-hospital cardiac arrests in China, 79.2% happened at home: learning chest compressions is first of all about helping your own family. Rules such as “do not enter the water yourself to rescue someone who is drowning” and “do not physically intervene in a fight” protect you from becoming a second casualty. Whether to help a stranger is your decision. Entries explain liability protections, ways to protect yourself and risks, rather than treating them as reasons you must intervene.

Mortality, time and energy, money and legal consequences are measured separately, without converting one into another. These four resources correspond to the four “What you gain” filters and are not compared across categories.

## Evidence grades

Every recommendation has an evidence grade:

| Grade | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| A | Quantifiable evidence from a meta-analysis, large cohort or randomized controlled trial, with specific figures such as HR, RR or percentage reductions. |
| B | Research support that is difficult to quantify, or evidence from a small sample or a single study. |
| C | The author’s experience or a commonly held view, without directly supporting literature. |

Of the 502 recommendations, 328 are grade A, 126 grade B and 48 grade C. Another 45 entries are marked as disputed, and 37 places carry TODO markers for verification. Disputed A- and B-grade entries are labeled “Disputed” and include opposing evidence. Citations supporting recommendations refer to primary sources: journal papers with DOI or PubMed links, or reports from official bodies such as WHO, CDC or the national statistics bureau. Local prices have separate retail and clinic sources; secondary price sources are labeled. Uncertain figures are marked “Needs verification.”

## Benefit-to-cost categories

An evidence grade addresses whether a figure is credible, not whether an action is worth taking. Each entry therefore also has a benefit magnitude and an outcome type. The search page combines these with the three cost dimensions to assign a benefit-to-cost category:

| Dimension | Values | How it is assigned |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outcome type | Lifespan / money / time and energy / personal freedom | The entry’s main benefit. **Different outcome types are not compared:** a 12% reduction in all-cause mortality and saving 500 yuan a year do not share a scale. |
| Benefit magnitude | Large / medium / small | Apply thresholds to the entry’s own “Benefit” figures where possible. Lifespan: a relative reduction ≥20% is large, 10–20% medium, and <10% or surrogate outcomes alone small. Money: tens of thousands of yuan is large, hundreds to thousands medium, tens small. Personal freedom: avoiding criminal liability is large, detention or administrative penalties medium, civil disputes small. Time and energy: hours saved daily is large, hours weekly medium, a one-off saving small. |
| Benefit relative to cost | Very high / high / moderate | Large benefit with zero cost in all three dimensions = very high; large benefit with low cost, or medium benefit with zero cost = high; all other combinations = moderate. |

Of 502 entries, 89 (18%) have very high benefit relative to cost, 249 (50%) high and 164 (33%) moderate. The broad middle category is deliberate: benefit magnitude itself has only three levels, so finer divisions would imply false precision.

**This category is the author’s judgment, not evidence.** It is effectively grade C and independent of the recommendation’s evidence grade. Grade A can have moderate benefit relative to cost: a phase III randomized trial found 97.2% efficacy for a shingles vaccine, but a self-paid course can cost about 485 USD in the US example or 550 EUR for the vaccine alone in the German example, while shingles is rarely fatal ([prices and conditions](translations/en/cost_examples.md)). Grade C can have very high benefit relative to cost, such as sending your itinerary to family before going abroad. “Moderate” does not mean “do not do it”: all included actions are recommended, but in this category you need to weigh the expense yourself.

## Understanding the numbers: glossary

The text uses plain language where possible, but research citations require some statistical terms. Check this table when something is unclear. On the search page, hovering over or selecting a term with a dotted underline also shows an explanation.

<details>
<summary>Expand 40 terms: all-cause mortality, HR, RR, 95% CI, meta-analysis, BMI, LPR, earnest money and advance payments…</summary>

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| All-cause mortality | The proportion of people who die from any cause over a period, regardless of cause. This book uses it to measure how long people live. The research term is all-cause mortality, abbreviated ACM. |
| HR | Hazard ratio: compares the instantaneous event rates among people who have not yet experienced the event. HR 0.87 means a 13% lower hazard than in the comparison group; HR 1.21 means a 21% higher hazard. It is not the ratio of the probabilities over the whole follow-up period. |
| RR | Relative risk: the ratio of the probabilities of an event in two groups over a specified period. As with HR, values below one indicate a lower measure and values above one a higher measure, but RR compares probabilities, not hazards. |
| OR | Odds ratio: the ratio of the odds of an event in two groups. It is close to RR for rare events; with common events it can make differences appear larger. |
| IRR | Incidence rate ratio: compares events per unit of person-time. Values below or above one indicate a lower or higher rate, respectively, as with other ratios; a rate is not a probability. |
| RaR | Rate ratio for events that can recur, such as falls. Values below or above one indicate a lower or higher rate, respectively, as with other ratios; this is not the proportion of people who fall. |
| Standardized mortality ratio | SMR: observed deaths in a group divided by the number expected from death rates in the general population of the same age. 5.86 means 5.86 times as many deaths. |
| Risk difference | Subtract one group’s event probability from the other’s, giving, for example, additional cases per thousand people. A ratio expresses a multiple; a risk difference expresses the absolute difference. |
| 95% CI | A 95% confidence interval expresses uncertainty around an estimate. Over repeated samples, 95% of intervals calculated by this procedure would contain the true value, assuming the statistical model holds. If a ratio interval includes 1, or a difference interval includes 0, chance may explain the difference; the text calls this “not statistically significant.” |
| RCT | Randomized controlled trial: people are randomly assigned to two groups, one receiving an intervention and the other not, and outcomes are compared. Best suited to establishing causation. |
| Meta-analysis | Statistical pooling of results from several studies to obtain an overall estimate; also called a meta analysis. |
| Cohort | A cohort study follows a group of people for years to see who experiences an event. It can establish associations, but not fully establish causation. |
| Observational | In an observational study, researchers observe without intervening. Confounding and reverse causation may affect results, so interpret the numbers cautiously. |
| Confounding | A third factor affects both the proposed cause and the outcome, making an association look causal. People who like vegetables may also exercise more. |
| Reverse causation | B causes A rather than A causing B. For example, serious illness may make people sleep more, rather than longer sleep causing early death. |
| d, g | Effect sizes: the difference between two group means in standard deviation units. 0.2 is considered small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large. |
| r | Correlation coefficient: how closely two things vary together, from −1 to 1. 0.1 is weak, 0.3 medium and 0.5 strong. |
| MET | A unit of exercise intensity. 1 MET is sitting quietly; brisk walking is about 3–4 MET. MET·h multiplies intensity by hours. |
| GRADE | An international system for rating evidence quality as high, moderate, low or very low. |
| Intention-to-screen analysis | Estimates the effect based on who was invited, rather than who actually attended screening. When some invitees do not attend, this can make the measured effect smaller than the effect among people who attend. |
| Pack-years | Smoking exposure: packs per day multiplied by years smoked. Thirty pack-years can mean one pack daily for thirty years. |
| BMI | Body mass index: weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. |
| LDL | Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, commonly called “bad” cholesterol. |
| HBsAg | Hepatitis B surface antigen; a positive result indicates hepatitis B infection. |
| HPV | Human papillomavirus. Persistent infection with some types can cause cervical cancer. |
| LDCT | Low-dose chest CT, with radiation exposure roughly one-fifth to one-tenth that of a standard CT. |
| PM2.5 | Airborne particles less than 2.5 micrometers in diameter. |
| NOVA | A food classification by degree of processing; ultra-processed foods are its fourth group. |
| LPR | Loan Prime Rate: China’s benchmark quoted lending rate, published on the 20th of each month. Mortgages and ceilings on private lending rates refer to it. |
| Final arbitration award | In the description of Chinese labor arbitration, the award takes effect directly and the employer cannot then bring a court action. |
| Crude marriage and divorce rates | Marriages and divorces registered during a year per thousand people. Divorces divided by marriages is a different measure, the divorce-to-marriage ratio; do not confuse them. |
| AED | Automated external defibrillator, often in a red or yellow emergency box in a public place. Turn it on and follow the voice prompts; it decides whether a shock is needed. |
| CPR | Cardiopulmonary resuscitation: pressing firmly on the chest during cardiac arrest to keep blood circulating. |
| 3C certification | China Compulsory Certification. Listed products cannot leave the factory or be sold without this mark. |
| ICP filing | Registering a website or app in China’s Ministry of Industry and Information Technology system before launch. |
| Multi-level protection | China’s cybersecurity multi-level protection system: classify systems by importance and implement the corresponding safeguards. |
| GPL | An open-source license. Using its code in a product generally requires providing source code when distributing that product. |
| Non-compete restriction | An agreement not to work for a competitor for a period after leaving a job. Under the Chinese rules described, the company must pay monthly compensation, and the maximum term is two years. |
| Subscribed capital contribution | Money promised when registering a company. It must be paid within the statutory period, not merely recorded on paper. |
| Earnest money and advance payment | Under the Chinese distinction, 定金 has a penalty: a recipient who breaches the agreement must repay double, and the amount is capped at 20% of the contract value. 订金 is only an advance payment, without that penalty. |

## Editorial notes

- The closing paragraph says 30 files, although the current contents list 31 chapters. The loading description has been clarified: the search page gets the chapter list from README and the text from book/.
- The glossary uses simplified definitions; the definitions of confidence intervals, hazards, probabilities and event rates have been clarified. A 95% confidence interval does not assign a 95% probability to an already calculated interval; it describes coverage of the true parameter when the procedure is repeated. HR, RR and OR are different measures. Legal definitions concern China and do not replace checking the complete rule.
- The salt example retains its contraindications and opposing evidence. The trial compared event rates in high-risk adults; it does not establish the same absolute benefit for everyone. Its salt substitute contained 25% potassium chloride, and retail products can differ. [Local prices](translations/en/cost_examples.md) give package prices rather than a comparison with regular salt. The example is not an individual prescription; [chapter 2, recommendation 5](translations/en/02_protect_your_health.md) explains the limitations.
