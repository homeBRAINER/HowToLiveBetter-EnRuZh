# 1. Avoid dying early

This chapter covers deaths from external causes, a few vaccines and screening tests with particularly strong evidence, and symptoms that warrant medical attention. Figures under “Benefit” come from randomized trials, large cohort studies or official statistics; individual entries explain the study design. Costs are the author’s rough estimates of market prices, intended for comparison rather than as reference data.

### 1. Wear your seat belt in the front and back seats

- Cost: No money; about 2 seconds each time you get in the car.
- In plain language: Buckle up in the front seat. The original describes this as roughly halving your chance of being killed or seriously injured in a crash, or reducing it by 60% in a light truck. Among US vehicle occupants killed in 2022 whose belt use was known, half were unrestrained. See the note below on grouping fatal and serious injuries together.
- Benefit: NHTSA estimates that seat belts reduce fatal injury risk by 45% for front-seat occupants of passenger cars and 60% for front-seat occupants of light trucks. In 2022, 50% of US passenger-vehicle occupants killed whose restraint use was known were unrestrained. WHO states that seat belts can reduce vehicle occupants’ risk of death by up to 50%. WHO estimated 248,099 road traffic deaths in China in 2021, or 17.4 per 100,000 people.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: NHTSA (2024). Occupant Protection in Passenger Vehicles: 2022 Data (DOT HS 813 573). <https://crashstats.nhtsa.dot.gov/Api/Public/ViewPublication/813573> ; WHO. Road traffic injuries. <https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/road-traffic-injuries> ; WHO GHO RS_196/RS_198 (China, 2021). <https://ghoapi.azureedge.net/api/RS_196?$filter=SpatialDim%20eq%20%27CHN%27>
- Notes: NHTSA’s 45% and 60% figures are official estimates based on US crash data, citing Kahane (2015), not a randomized trial. Back-seat occupants need belts too: in the same report, 60% of second-row occupants killed whose belt use was known were unrestrained. WHO’s figure for China is a modeled estimate, several times higher than recorded traffic-police deaths; the definitions and methods differ. This entry uses WHO’s estimate. Editorial clarification: NHTSA’s figures here concern fatal injuries. The original plain-language summary also applies them to serious injuries, which the figures cited here do not establish. The WHO page was updated on 20 July 2026; the original cites it as a 2025 publication.

### 2. Wear a helmet on a motorcycle or e-bike, and fasten the chin strap

- Cost: 100–300 Chinese yuan for a helmet; about 5 seconds to put it on before each ride.
- In plain language: A helmet helps protect your head in a crash. A Cochrane review of motorcycle riders reported estimated reductions of about 42% in deaths and 69% in head injuries. These figures concern motorcycles and cannot be applied directly to e-bikes. Fasten the chin strap: the original emphasizes this by saying that leaving it undone is like not wearing a helmet at all.
- Benefit: In the Cochrane meta-analysis, helmet use among motorcycle riders was associated with lower odds of death: odds ratio (OR) 0.58, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 0.50–0.68. For head injury, the OR was 0.31 (95% CI 0.25–0.38). The original describes these as risk reductions of 42% and 69%, respectively.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Liu BC et al. (2008). Helmets for preventing injury in motorcycle riders. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD004333.pub3>
- Notes: The research concerns motorcycle riders. The author extends the advice to e-bikes based on a similar mechanism of injury. A helmet hanging from the handlebars will not protect your head: wear it and fasten the strap. Editorial clarification: an odds ratio is not a risk ratio. An OR of 0.58 does not automatically mean a 42% reduction in an individual’s probability of death. The figures and intervals are retained, with more precise wording for the measure.

### 3. Install smoke alarms, and add a carbon monoxide alarm if you heat your home with coal or gas

- Cost: 30–100 Chinese yuan for a smoke alarm and 50–150 yuan for a carbon monoxide alarm. The author allows for a yearly battery change. Editorial clarification: replacement schedules depend on the device; follow the manufacturer’s instructions for alarms with sealed batteries.
- In plain language: The original describes a working smoke alarm as reducing the chance of dying in a residential fire by about 60%. Around four in ten people are asleep when the fire starts; do not rely on the smell waking you. If you burn coal indoors or use gas heating in winter, add a carbon monoxide alarm: China recorded about 11,500 carbon monoxide poisoning deaths in 2018 alone, with roughly seven in ten deaths during the three winter months occurring at home. See the note below on the “60%” estimate.
- Benefit: A North Carolina case-control study found an association between a working smoke alarm and lower odds of death: OR 0.39 (95% CI 0.18–0.83). In US fatal fires in occupied residential buildings in 2018–2020, smoke alarms were absent in 24% and occupants were asleep when the fire started in 41%. China recorded 11,523 carbon monoxide poisoning deaths in 2018. The shares occurring at home in December, January and February were 72.59%, 67.42% and 66.48%, respectively.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Marshall SW et al. (1998). Fatal residential fires: who dies and who survives? JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.279.20.1633> ; USFA (2022). Fatal Fires in Residential Buildings (2018–2020), Topical Fire Report Series 22(2). <https://www.usfa.fema.gov/downloads/pdf/statistics/v22i2.pdf> ; You J et al. (2020). Number of Deaths due to Carbon Monoxide Poisoning by Month and by Place of Death — China, 2018. China CDC Weekly. <https://doi.org/10.46234/ccdcw2020.008> ; Editorial addition on maintenance: USFA. Smoke Alarms. <https://www.usfa.fema.gov/prevention/home-fires/prepare-for-fire/smoke-alarms/>
- Notes: The author cites one case-control study for smoke alarms, not a randomized trial, and assigns grade B. The original does not cite a study measuring the effect of carbon monoxide alarms themselves on mortality; the author bases that advice on the concentration of poisoning deaths at home in winter. Editorial clarification: an OR of 0.39 does not mean a 61% reduction in the probability of death for every household. The original’s “about 60% lower risk” wording is retained above as the author’s summary; the exact measure is given under Benefit. Maintain alarms according to their instructions: USFA distinguishes alarms with replaceable batteries from those with sealed batteries.
