# 6. What is not worth it

> Complete chapter translation: the introduction and all 16 recommendations. Editorial clarifications and regional notes are labeled separately.

This chapter collects things that seem inexpensive and effective but have shown little benefit, no established benefit, or harm for the purposes discussed. The order combines how much people spend on them with the strength of the evidence: items nearer the top are worth reviewing first. The qualifications and exceptions matter; a lack of evidence is not always proof of no effect.

### 1. Don’t take a multivitamin to live longer or prevent cardiovascular disease
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Recurring spending and the feeling that you cannot do without a daily pill. Price examples checked on September 21, 2026: about 5 USD for 125 Equate Adults 50+ tablets at [Walmart](https://www.walmart.com/cp/equate-brand-shop/1083166?povid=Vitaminscp_topnav_brands_equate), or 3 EUR for 100 Mivolis A–Z tablets at [dm in Germany](https://www.dm.de/p/d/3104283/mivolis-a-z-komplett-tabletten-100-st). These are different products and package sizes, not equivalent treatment courses. Prices exclude delivery and any US sales tax; listings may be cached. Annual spending depends on the product and how often you buy it.
- In plain language: More than 14,000 male doctors took part in a trial lasting about eleven years. Multivitamins did not significantly reduce cardiovascular events or deaths. Combining nine trials with over 50,000 participants did not establish a mortality benefit either. You can save the recurring expense. Treating a diagnosed deficiency or taking folic acid during pregnancy is a different matter.
- Benefit: In PHS II, 14,641 male doctors were followed for a median of 11.2 years: the hazard ratio (HR) was 1.01 for major cardiovascular events (95% CI 0.91–1.10) and 0.94 for all-cause mortality (0.88–1.02). The USPSTF’s 2022 assessment covered 9 randomised controlled trials (RCTs) with 51,550 participants and found no significant association between multivitamins and all-cause mortality; its I statement means insufficient evidence. The appeal is a low price per pill and the idea that one tablet covers every nutritional need.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Sesso HD et al. (2012). Multivitamins in the prevention of cardiovascular disease in men: the Physicians’ Health Study II randomized controlled trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.14805>; US Preventive Services Task Force (2022). Vitamin, Mineral, and Multivitamin Supplementation to Prevent Cardiovascular Disease and Cancer. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2022.8970>
- Notes: An important qualification: the cancer analysis in the same PHS II cohort found an HR of 0.92 for total cancer incidence (0.86–0.998; P=0.04); cancer mortality was not significantly reduced, HR 0.88 (0.77–1.01). See Gaziano JM et al. (2012), JAMA, <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.14641>. The incidence effect was small and came from a population of male doctors. People with diagnosed deficiencies, pregnant people who need folic acid, and people with severely unbalanced diets may need supplements on clinical advice. **Clarification:** insufficient evidence does not prove that the effect is exactly zero. The book’s grade A describes the study design; it is not the USPSTF’s recommendation grade.

### 2. Don’t take ordinary fish-oil supplements to prevent cardiovascular disease
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Recurring purchases. A checked German example is about 3.50 EUR for 60 Mivolis Omega-3 1000 capsules at [dm](https://www.dm.de/gesundheit/wohlbefinden-und-staerkung/herz-kreislauf-und-cholesterin), listed at 3.45 EUR on September 21, 2026. A comparable US price in USD has not been verified. The amount of fish oil on a label is not necessarily the amount of EPA plus DHA; this package is a price example, not an equivalent of the trial formulation or an annual treatment estimate.
- In plain language: Two large trials, one with about 26,000 adults and one with about 15,000 people with diabetes, tested 1 gram a day of marine omega-3 supplements. Neither established a reduction in its main cardiovascular outcome or in deaths. Evidence that eating fish is beneficial does not automatically establish a benefit from fish-oil capsules. Skipping unnecessary capsules saves their recurring cost.
- Benefit: VITAL included 25,871 participants taking 1 g/day, with a median follow-up of 5.3 years: major cardiovascular events HR 0.92 (95% CI 0.80–1.06), all-cause mortality HR 1.02 (0.90–1.15). ASCEND included 15,480 people with diabetes taking 1 g/day, with mean follow-up of 7.4 years: serious vascular events RR 0.97 (0.87–1.08), all-cause mortality RR 0.95 (0.86–1.05). The sales pitch equates the benefits of eating fish with those of taking capsules.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Manson JE et al. (2019). Marine n-3 Fatty Acids and Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease and Cancer. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1811403>; ASCEND Study Collaborative Group (2018). Effects of n-3 Fatty Acid Supplements in Diabetes Mellitus. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1804989>
- Notes: There is a distinct positive result: REDUCE-IT included 8,179 people with established cardiovascular disease or diabetes, taking statins and with triglycerides of 135–499 mg/dL. With 4 g/day of prescription purified EPA, the primary outcome occurred in 17.2% versus 22.0%; HR 0.75 (0.68–0.83). Bhatt DL et al. (2019), NEJM, <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1812792>. This was a high-dose prescription treatment for high-risk patients, not ordinary fish-oil supplements for everyone. Eating fish itself is outside this recommendation.

### 3. Don’t take vitamin D solely to live longer if you have no deficiency
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: The purchase may be inexpensive, but it can create a false sense that you have taken care of prevention. For example, [dm in Germany](https://www.dm.de/p/d/1582081/doppelherz-vitamin-d3-2000-i-e-mini-tabletten-60-st) lists 60 Doppelherz vitamin D3 2000 IU tablets at 2.95 EUR, about 3 EUR per pack, checked September 21, 2026. A comparable US price in USD has not been verified. This describes a package, not a recommended dose or treatment duration.
- In plain language: A trial with about 26,000 people followed for roughly five years did not establish that vitamin D prevented cancer, major cardiovascular events or death. Another gave monthly supplements to about 21,000 older adults for five years and found no significant mortality benefit: 5.3% died in the supplement group and 5.1% in the placebo group. These trials did not specifically recruit people with confirmed deficiency. Deficiency, osteoporosis and very limited sunlight call for individual advice, not this blanket prevention claim.
- Benefit: VITAL included 25,871 participants taking 2000 IU/day, with median follow-up of 5.3 years: invasive cancer HR 0.96 (95% CI 0.88–1.06), major cardiovascular events HR 0.97 (0.85–1.12), all-cause mortality HR 0.99 (0.87–1.12). D-Health assigned 21,315 Australians aged 60 or older to 60,000 IU/month or placebo for 5 years: all-cause mortality HR 1.04 (0.93–1.18), with mortality of 5.3% versus 5.1%. The tempting but unproven inference is that, because lower vitamin D levels predict higher mortality in observational studies, taking a supplement must reduce it.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Manson JE et al. (2019). Vitamin D Supplements and Prevention of Cancer and Cardiovascular Disease. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1809944>; Neale RE et al. (2022). The D-Health Trial: a randomised controlled trial of the effect of vitamin D on mortality. Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587(21)00345-4>
- Notes: Neither trial specifically selected people with genuine vitamin D deficiency, so these findings do not settle how to treat deficiency. Diagnosed deficiency, osteoporosis, prolonged lack of sunlight, infancy and other specific circumstances may still warrant supplementation on clinical advice. This entry concerns all-cause mortality and cancer/cardiovascular outcomes. **Clarification:** “not selected for deficiency” does not mean every participant was confirmed to have adequate levels. The monthly regimen is a research intervention, not a self-treatment instruction.

### 4. Don’t take antioxidant supplements to prevent cancer: beta-carotene, vitamin E or vitamin A
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Recurring spending; in smokers, some regimens also increase lung-cancer risk. No comparable local price for the specific high-dose formulations has been verified in USD or EUR. Their price would not remove that risk.
- In plain language: Some of these supplements can do harm. A review of 78 trials with nearly 300,000 people found a small mortality increase in the trials at low risk of bias. In two large trials involving smokers, beta-carotene, alone or combined with vitamin A, was associated with 18%–28% more lung cancers and 8%–17% more deaths. Smokers and people exposed to asbestos have particular reason to avoid these cancer-prevention regimens.
- Benefit: In a Cochrane review of 78 RCTs with 296,707 participants, the overall random-effects estimate for mortality was RR 1.02 (95% CI 0.98–1.05), which was not statistically significant. The estimate in trials at low risk of bias was RR 1.04 (1.01–1.07); for beta-carotene, RR 1.05 (1.01–1.09); for vitamin E, RR 1.03 (1.00–1.05). ATBC included 29,133 male smokers and tested beta-carotene at 20 mg/day: lung-cancer incidence increased by 18% (3%–36%) and mortality by 8% (1%–16%). CARET included 18,314 smokers or people exposed to asbestos: lung cancer RR 1.28 (1.04–1.57), all-cause mortality RR 1.17 (1.03–1.33). The appeal rests on treating “antioxidant,” “anti-ageing” and “cancer prevention” as interchangeable ideas.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Bjelakovic G et al. (2012). Antioxidant supplements for prevention of mortality in healthy participants and patients with various diseases. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD007176.pub2>; The Alpha-Tocopherol, Beta Carotene Cancer Prevention Study Group (1994). The effect of vitamin E and beta carotene on the incidence of lung cancer and other cancers in male smokers. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199404143301501>; Omenn GS et al. (1996). Effects of a combination of beta carotene and vitamin A on lung cancer and cardiovascular disease. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199605023341802>
- Notes: In 2022, the USPSTF gave beta-carotene and vitamin E a D recommendation: do not use them for this preventive purpose. Evidence of beta-carotene-related harm is particularly relevant to smokers and people exposed to asbestos. Antioxidants in fruit and vegetables are outside this recommendation. **Clarification:** CARET tested a combination of beta-carotene and vitamin A. The overall random-effects estimate and the low-bias analysis are different results; the former alone does not demonstrate increased mortality.

### 5. Don’t count on glucosamine or chondroitin to relieve knee osteoarthritis
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Recurring supplement purchases. A comparable local course price in USD or EUR has not been verified; formulations, doses and pack sizes differ. Compare the full recurring bill, not just the price of one tablet.
- In plain language: In a 24-week trial involving 1,583 people, 60.1% of the placebo group met the pain-response criterion. Glucosamine improved that figure by only 3.9 percentage points and chondroitin by 5.3 points; neither difference was statistically significant. Paying for a supplement does not mean it caused the improvement you experienced.
- Benefit: GAIT included 1,583 people with knee osteoarthritis over 24 weeks. The placebo response rate was 60.1%. Glucosamine exceeded it by 3.9 percentage points (P=0.30), chondroitin by 5.3 points (P=0.17), and the combination by 6.5 points (P=0.09); none of these differences was significant. Celecoxib, the active comparator, exceeded placebo by 10.0 points (P=0.008). “Providing the raw material for cartilage” sounds directly relevant, while a substantial response in the placebo group makes individual improvement easy to misattribute.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Clegg DO et al. (2006). Glucosamine, chondroitin sulfate, and the two in combination for painful knee osteoarthritis. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa052771>
- Notes: A qualification: in the subgroup with moderate-to-severe pain at baseline, response was 79.2% with the combination versus 54.3% with placebo (P=0.002). The researchers explicitly treated this as exploratory. Weight management where appropriate and muscle-strengthening exercise are evidence-based options; see [chapter 2](02_protect_your_health.md). **Clarification:** a 60.1% response in the placebo group is not a measured 60.1% placebo effect. Natural fluctuations, regression to the mean and other influences can also contribute.

### 6. Don’t rely on vitamin C to prevent colds
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: A recurring expense even when each serving is cheap. Examples checked September 21, 2026: about 8 USD for 30 Equate 1000 mg drink-mix sachets, listed from 7.92 USD at [Walmart](https://www.walmart.com/c/kp/equate-vitamin-c); 0.50 EUR for 20 Mivolis 240 mg effervescent tablets at [dm in Germany](https://www.dm.de/p/d/3104290/mivolis-vitamin-c-brausetabletten-20-st). Different strengths and ingredients make these price examples, not equivalent courses. Annual cost depends on purchases; US tax and delivery are additional where applicable.
- In plain language: Across 29 comparisons involving 11,306 people, taking vitamin C regularly did not meaningfully lower the risk of catching a cold in the general population. Starting after symptoms began did not produce a consistent benefit either. Regular use did shorten adult colds by about 8%—roughly half a day for a week-long cold. Decide whether that modest benefit is worth the recurring cost. Marathon runners and soldiers exercising in subarctic conditions were an exception: their risk was roughly halved.
- Benefit: The Cochrane review included 29 comparisons with 11,306 participants. In general-community trials, regular vitamin C gave a cold-incidence RR of 0.97 (95% CI 0.94–1.00), with no meaningful prevention benefit. Trials starting treatment after onset found no consistent effect. The appeal is low cost, a pleasant sweet-sour drink and a prevention claim repeated for half a century.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Hemilä H, Chalker E (2013). Vitamin C for preventing and treating the common cold. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000980.pub4>
- Notes: A real but different benefit: regular supplementation shortened cold duration by about 8% in adults (3%–12%) and 14% in children (7%–21%). Roughly half a day is an illustration, not a fixed saving for every cold. In marathon runners, skiers and soldiers on subarctic exercises, 5 trials with 598 participants gave RR 0.48 (0.35–0.64). Most people are not in those extreme-exertion settings. Prevention and shorter illness duration are separate outcomes.

### 7. Don’t buy whole-body PET-CT or tumour-marker screening packages when you have no symptoms
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: The scan or laboratory package, plus possible repeat tests, biopsies and surgery after false alarms. PET-CT also exposes you to ionising radiation. A comparable all-in self-pay price has not been verified in USD or EUR; prices depend on the procedure, provider and coverage. A laboratory package and a PET-CT scan are not interchangeable purchases.
- In plain language: More testing can mean more findings that do not help you. Among 1,192 people without symptoms who had whole-body CT, 86% had at least one abnormal finding and 37% were advised to have further assessment; most findings were described as benign. Ovarian-cancer screening trials found no mortality benefit. Across trials, 0.2%–3.25% underwent surgery after false-positive results, and up to 15% of those operated on had serious complications. Pay attention to proven, targeted screening instead.
- Benefit: The USPSTF gives a D recommendation against ovarian-cancer screening with CA-125 and ultrasound in asymptomatic women without a known high-risk hereditary cancer syndrome. In PLCO, ovarian-cancer mortality was 0.34% with screening versus 0.29% with usual care, RR 1.18 (95% CI 0.82–1.71): no demonstrated benefit, and no statistically established increase either. Across trials, false-positive results led to surgery in 0.2%–3.25% of participants; up to 15% of those operations had serious complications. In a descriptive whole-body CT study of 1,192 asymptomatic people, 86% had at least one abnormality and 37% were advised to have further evaluation; most findings were described as benign. “Check everything at once and catch it early” is an appealing but incomplete argument.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: US Preventive Services Task Force (2018). Screening for Ovarian Cancer: US Preventive Services Task Force Recommendation Statement. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2017.21926>; official recommendation: <https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/ovarian-cancer-screening>; Furtado CD et al. (2005). Whole-body CT screening: spectrum of findings and recommendations in 1192 patients. Radiology. <https://doi.org/10.1148/radiol.2372041741>
- Notes: The ovarian-screening evidence includes RCTs; the whole-body CT evidence here is descriptive, corresponding to grade B. Proven screening for colorectal, cervical and breast cancer, and low-dose chest CT for eligible high-risk people, is a separate matter; see [chapter 1](01_avoid_dying_early.md). PET-CT used to investigate symptoms or manage diagnosed cancer is outside this entry. **Clarification:** an abnormal or benign finding is not automatically a false-positive cancer result. The cited CT study did not test PET-CT or show that every tumour-marker test has the same balance of benefits and harms.

### 8. Don’t expect a fitness tracker to do the work of losing weight
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: A device purchase and the attention spent checking it every day. No like-for-like tracker prices in USD and EUR have been verified here. What matters is the cost you can avoid if weight loss is your only reason for buying one.
- In plain language: In a trial of 471 adults with overweight or obesity, both groups received a lifestyle programme. After two years, the group given a wearable had lost 3.5 kg; the group without it had lost 5.9 kg. Buying a tracker is not a prerequisite for changing your habits or losing weight.
- Benefit: In IDEA, 471 adults with overweight or obesity were followed for 24 months. Alongside a lifestyle intervention, the wearable group lost 3.5 kg (95% CI 2.6–4.5), compared with 5.9 kg (5.0–6.8) in the group without the wearable. The difference was 2.4 kg (1.0–3.7; P=0.002). The pitch combines “you can only manage what you measure” with a one-off purchase.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Jakicic JM et al. (2016). Effect of Wearable Technology Combined With a Lifestyle Intervention on Long-term Weight Loss: The IDEA Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2016.12858>
- Notes: This was one trial using an upper-arm device from the early 2010s. It cannot establish that every wearable is useless. Physical activity has benefits; see [chapter 2](02_protect_your_health.md). The narrower point is that a device is not an essential weight-loss expense. **Clarification:** this was a multicomponent lifestyle programme, not a test showing that exercise alone accounts for all the weight lost.

### 9. Don’t pay an organic-food premium solely for a promised health benefit
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: A possible price premium. A price of 1–3 times the conventional alternative is an illustrative range, not a verified rule for US or European shops. Compare the same food, quantity and quality locally.
- In plain language: A review of 17 human studies and 223 studies of food composition found no strong evidence that organic food is substantially more nutritious. Only three human studies measured clinical outcomes, and they found no significant differences. Organic produce was 30 percentage points less likely to have detectable pesticide residues, but detecting a residue is not the same as exceeding a legal limit. Eating fruit and vegetables matters regardless of the organic label.
- Benefit: The systematic review included 17 human studies and 223 studies of nutrients and contaminants in food. It found no strong evidence of substantially greater nutritional value in organic food. Only 3 human studies assessed clinical outcomes—allergies and symptomatic infections—with no significant group differences. The risk of detectable pesticide residues was 30 percentage points lower in organic produce, but detection is not the same as an excessive level. “Pesticide-free means safer, therefore healthier” skips several unproven steps.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Smith-Spangler C et al. (2012). Are organic foods safer or healthier than conventional alternatives?: a systematic review. Ann Intern Med. <https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-157-5-201209040-00007>
- Notes: Very few human studies measured health outcomes: a benefit was not established, rather than disproved. Environmental effects, animal welfare and taste are outside this comparison. The benefits of eating fruit and vegetables do not depend on choosing organic. **Clarification:** “organic” does not mean that no pesticides can have been used; the residue finding itself does not prove a clinical advantage.

### 10. Don’t spend heavily on supplements, herbal pastes or tonics to “rebalance” your body
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Repeated or expensive purchases, sometimes with a large gift-packaging markup. Products are too heterogeneous for a defensible single price range in USD or EUR; no comparable local range has been verified.
- In plain language: China’s health-food labelling rule requires a warning occupying at least 20% of the main display panel: these products are not medicines and cannot replace disease treatment. Their regulatory category does not establish that they can treat an illness. The first six entries in this chapter examine common ingredients—multivitamins, fish oil, vitamin D, antioxidants, glucosamine and vitamin C. They do not support a blanket promise of better health; some have specific benefits or risks.
- Benefit: China’s State Administration for Market Regulation requires the non-medicine warning to occupy at least 20% of the main display panel. It describes health foods as intended to supplement nutrients, maintain or improve health, or reduce disease risk factors; this is distinct from authorisation to treat disease. The first 6 entries give RCT results for the most common ingredients: multivitamins, fish oil, vitamin D, antioxidants, glucosamine and vitamin C. “It does not treat disease; it rebalances you” can be too vague to test, while gift buying is often driven by social obligation rather than evidence.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: State Administration for Market Regulation of China (2019). Press briefing on the guidelines for health-food warning statements and the management of ingredient and permitted-function catalogues. <https://www.samr.gov.cn/tssps/sjdt/tpxw/art/2023/art_4b658b824b1b4b0ba57c09a56cc93aad.html>
- Notes: Lack of supporting evidence is not proof that every product is ineffective; hence grade C. For a product with identified ingredients, check the relevant studies in the preceding entries. If you have a diagnosed deficiency or are receiving treatment, follow clinical advice. **Regional clarification:** the 20% requirement described here is Chinese law, not a US or European labelling rule. **Evidence clarification:** the first six entries do not all show the same result; they include limited benefits, uncertain estimates and evidence of harm.

### 11. Don’t take probiotics indefinitely for general health when you have no gut problem
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Recurring spending. Comparable US and European prices in USD and EUR have not been verified for the same strains, dose and pack size. A low daily price can still become a continuing expense.
- In plain language: A review of 45 studies in healthy adults did not establish lasting changes in gut microbiota or better blood lipid levels. Changes in the supplemented bacteria tended to be temporary. This is not a reason to reject every strain for every purpose: specific uses, such as some cases of antibiotic-associated diarrhoea, have their own evidence.
- Benefit: A review of 45 studies in healthy adults did not support persistent microbiota changes or improved lipid profiles. Supplement-associated bacterial changes could be transient rather than a permanent “reset” after the course ends. The appeal comes from a broad promise of “gut health” and a small price for each daily serving.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Khalesi S et al. (2019). A review of probiotic supplementation in healthy adults: helpful or hype? Eur J Clin Nutr. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-018-0135-9>
- Notes: The same review reported improvements in some measures, including stool consistency and vaginal lactobacilli. Evidence for particular strains and indications, including antibiotic-associated diarrhoea, must be considered separately. This entry concerns routine supplementation in otherwise healthy people. **Clarification:** the review also reported possible benefits for some immune responses and bowel function. It did not establish that every microbiota measure returns to baseline immediately after stopping, or that all probiotic use in healthy adults is useless.

### 12. Don’t force yourself to take cold showers to “boost immunity”
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No purchase is needed, but there may be 30–90 seconds of discomfort each day and an effort to persist in winter. Cold exposure can pose risks, especially with cardiovascular disease.
- In plain language: In a trial of 3,018 people, those assigned to finish their shower with cold water reported 29% fewer days off work because of sickness. They did not report significantly fewer days feeling ill. That does not show that they caught fewer illnesses or had stronger immunity. A 2025 review of 11 trials found a short-lived increase in inflammatory markers after cold-water exposure, without a clear change in immune markers. Enjoying a cold shower is one thing; forcing yourself for an unproven health return is another.
- Benefit: In the Dutch RCT, 3,018 people were assigned to end a warm shower with 30, 60 or 90 seconds of cold water, or to shower as usual. Sickness-absence days fell by 29% (IRR 0.71), but illness days did not differ significantly. Quality of life, work productivity and anxiety showed no clinically relevant group differences. A 2025 systematic review of 11 RCTs with 3,177 participants found increased inflammatory markers immediately and 1 hour after cold-water immersion, no clear immune-marker change, and a stress reduction only at 12 hours. The evidence was limited by few trials and small samples. The idea feels economical because it costs nothing and effort or discomfort can be mistaken for effectiveness.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Buijze GA et al. (2016). The Effect of Cold Showering on Health and Work: A Randomized Controlled Trial. PLOS ONE. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0161749>; Cain T et al. (2025). Effects of cold-water immersion on health and wellbeing: A systematic review and meta-analysis. PLOS ONE. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0317615>
- Notes: The 29% reduction in sickness absence was statistically significant. Fewer absence days without fewer illness days could reflect working while unwell or a different threshold for staying home, but this is an interpretation, not a demonstrated mechanism. Recovery after athletic training is a separate question. **Clarification:** the trial measured days absent, not the number of sick-leave episodes; illness days include illness without absence. It excluded people with serious comorbidity and does not prove cold showers are harmless for everyone. A review largely about immersion is not a direct test of every shower routine. Cold showers are optional, not an established immunity investment.

### 13. Don’t buy “detox,” colon-cleansing or enzyme drinks, or alkaline water, for promised health protection
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: The product or programme, sometimes bought repeatedly. No comparable local package prices in USD or EUR have been verified. Fasting-based detox programmes can also cause low blood glucose or inadequate nutrition.
- In plain language: Commercial detox is a large industry with little convincing clinical evidence behind its dietary claims. A 2015 review found no randomised trials of commercial detox diets. A separate review screened 8,278 references on dietary acid load, alkaline water and cancer; only one study qualified, and it found no association. Fasting for a “cleanse” can leave you undernourished or hypoglycaemic.
- Benefit: The 2015 critical review found scant clinical support for detox diets and no RCTs of commercial detox diets within its search. The systematic review of alkaline diets/water and cancer screened 8,278 references; only 1 study met its inclusion criteria and it found no association. Promoting these products to prevent or treat cancer was not justified by that evidence. The pitch works because “toxins accumulate and must be removed” and “an acidic body causes cancer” sound like physiology without establishing either claim.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Klein AV, Kiat H (2015). Detox diets for toxin elimination and weight management: a critical review of the evidence. J Hum Nutr Diet. <https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.12286>; Fenton TR, Huang T (2016). Systematic review of the association between dietary acid load, alkaline water and cancer. BMJ Open. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2015-010438>
- Notes: This is a lack-of-supporting-evidence claim, hence grade C. The liver and kidneys normally process and remove metabolic waste; without a relevant disease, a commercial “detox” is not an additional requirement. **Clarification:** the statements about absent trials describe the reviews’ search periods, not a new exhaustive search of all research today. Medical treatment for poisoning or organ failure is a different matter.

### 14. Don’t force yourself to drink just to reach “eight glasses a day”
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No extra purchase is necessary; the costs can be time, attention and more trips to the toilet at night.
- In plain language: A review tracing the “eight glasses” rule found no scientific study supporting that fixed target. Surveys of food and fluid intake in thousands of people pointed out how much water already comes from food and other drinks. Thirst and urine colour can be useful everyday clues, but they are not a complete assessment for everyone. Heat, strenuous exercise, a history of kidney stones or reduced thirst in older age may call for planned fluid intake.
- Benefit: The review of “8×8”—8 glasses of 8 US fluid ounces each day—found no scientific support for that universal rule. Surveys of food and fluid intake in thousands of adults suggested that this much additional water was not necessary, because food and other beverages already supply substantial water. It sounds sensible because water is cheap, the number is memorable and “drink more” appears harmless in almost any context.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Valtin H (2002). “Drink at least eight glasses of water a day.” Really? Is there scientific evidence for “8 x 8”? Am J Physiol Regul Integr Comp Physiol. <https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpregu.00365.2002>
- Notes: The point is that a fixed number lacks support, not that drinking extra water is always harmful. Thirst and urine colour are practical clues for many healthy adults. People working in heat, exercising strenuously, managing kidney stones or experiencing diminished thirst in older age may need to drink proactively; they fall outside the simple rule. **Clarification:** 8×8 is approximately 1.9 litres, not eight litres. If a clinician has set a fluid target or restriction, including for heart or kidney disease, follow that individual plan; forcing excessive intake is not risk-free.

### 15. Don’t pay for fortune-telling, tarot or astrology to make consequential decisions
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No purchase is needed; you give up a shortcut for deciding what to do.
- In plain language: In a classic classroom experiment, everyone received the same supposed personal profile, yet students generally found it accurate. That helps explain why a reading can feel uncannily specific. The costs can go well beyond a small entertainment budget. A fraud group in Xixian, Henan, China, drew people in with free palm readings, then charged for incense and “offering packages” costing up to 3,888 CNY. More than 50 people were victimised, over 2 million CNY was recovered, and the ringleader received an eleven-year prison sentence. Paying a modest amount for entertainment is different from using a reading to decide whether to quit a job, marry or invest.
- Benefit: In Forer’s classroom experiment, students completed a personality test and received identical “individual” analyses, which they generally judged accurate. Acceptance by the recipient or interpreter does not validate an interpretation—the Barnum effect helps explain the experience. Carlson’s Nature paper used two double-blind tests to examine whether birth charts accurately described personality. In the official Xixian case, free palm readings led to incense charges and packages priced up to 3,888 CNY; there were more than 50 victims and over 2 million CNY in recovered proceeds. The principal offender was sentenced to eleven years’ imprisonment and fined 100,000 CNY.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Forer BR (1949). The fallacy of personal validation: A classroom demonstration of gullibility. Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/h0059240>; Carlson S (1985). A double-blind test of astrology. Nature. <https://doi.org/10.1038/318419a0>; Supreme People’s Procuratorate of China (2026). Xixian, Henan: three years of investigation into online fortune-telling fraud groups. <https://www.spp.gov.cn/zdgz/202607/t20260728_732935.shtml>
- Notes: Both papers are individual studies. Only their abstracts have been checked for this entry; TODO: verify Forer’s mean ratings and Carlson’s astrologer hit rates from accessible full texts. The APA and Nature pages did not provide those figures outside their paywalls. The question is predictive validity, not a judgement about faith. A modest entertainment budget is outside this warning; using a reading to decide about employment, marriage or investments is the concern. **Regional clarification:** the fraud amounts and sentence describe a specific Chinese case and remain in CNY. They are neither local prices nor a statement about US or European penalties.

### 16. Don’t pay for blue-light-filtering glasses to “protect your eyesight,” or assume a few months at a screen will ruin your eyes
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No purchase is required; you avoid an optional lens surcharge. A comparable surcharge in USD or EUR has not been verified: it depends on the lens, prescription and retailer, so a whole pair’s price is not a like-for-like figure.
- In plain language: A Cochrane review of 17 trials found that blue-light-filtering lenses may make no difference to short-term eye strain compared with ordinary lenses. They probably do not change best-corrected visual acuity, and the review found no evidence establishing protection of the macula. Screen-related soreness and dryness are often temporary and do not by themselves mean permanent damage. Intense optical exposure, including welding arcs, ultraviolet radiation and lasers, is a different hazard requiring appropriate protection.
- Benefit: The 2023 Cochrane review included 17 RCTs with 5–156 participants, followed for less than 1 day to 5 weeks. Limited data, different populations and follow-up periods prevented a pooled analysis. Compared with non-filtering lenses, subjective eye strain at less than one week may differ little or not at all, with low-certainty evidence: one 120-person trial found a mean difference of 9.76 units (95% CI −33.95 to 53.47). Critical flicker-fusion frequency may also show little or no difference, with low certainty. Best-corrected visual acuity probably does not change: mean difference 0.00 logMAR (95% CI −0.02 to 0.02; 1 trial, 156 participants; moderate certainty). Effects on daytime alertness are uncertain: 2 trials, 42 participants, very low certainty. The review found no evidence establishing macular protection.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Singh S, Downie LE, Anderson AJ, Keller PR, White SJ, Ang M, Wolffsohn JS (2023). Blue-light filtering spectacle lenses for visual performance, sleep, and macular health in adults. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 8, CD013244. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD013244.pub2>
- Notes: Digital eye strain and dry-eye symptoms can include soreness, dryness and blurred vision after prolonged use; breaks and blinking can help, and symptoms are often reversible. This clinical context is separate from the lens review and is graded C here. Intense light can injure the eye; use the right goggles or face shield for the exposure, not blue-light-filtering spectacle lenses. See [chapter 19, entry 11](../../book/19-在职离职和工伤.md). Photokeratitis is recognised as an occupational disease in China; that classification is not a claim about every country. **Clinical clarification:** welding-related ultraviolet photokeratitis usually heals within 24–72 hours; it is not invariably irreversible. Some injuries, including retinal laser injuries, can be permanent. See the [American Academy of Ophthalmology’s EyeWiki](https://eyewiki.aao.org/Photokeratitis). Persistent discomfort or genuinely declining vision needs an eye examination, not just different lenses. For childhood and adolescent myopia prevention, see [chapter 30, entries 4, 10 and 7](../../book/30-上学以后的孩子.md).
