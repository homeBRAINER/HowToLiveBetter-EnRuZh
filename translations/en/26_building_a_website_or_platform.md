# 26. Build a website or platform: permits, registration and servers

This chapter answers a practical question: what business does your website or app conduct under the law, which permits do you need, where should you host the server, and who is responsible for what users post and the money they pay? The outcomes are personal freedom and money. Criminal restrictions come first, followed by administrative permits, everyday platform duties, and finally practical judgments such as choosing a server. [Chapter 11](11_legal_risks_for_tech_workers.md) covers legal risks for employed technical staff; [chapter 12](12_starting_a_business.md) covers company registration and tax returns. Those topics are not repeated here. For a comparison of permits and a server-selection decision table, see [the detailed platform guide](../../docs/做平台要办哪些证.md).

The introduction and entries **1–6 of 11** are translated. [Entries 7–11 are available in Chinese](../../book/26-做一个网站或平台.md). The Chinese rules below concern businesses within their stated jurisdiction. US, EU and UK guidance appears separately in the notes; Chinese license names, capital requirements and penalties are not local requirements in those markets.

### 1. Have a licensed payment provider settle directly with sellers; keep buyers' money out of the platform's own account
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=中 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=自由 -->
- Cost: Reading the rules and choosing the settlement arrangement costs $0 or €0. Use a licensed provider's split-settlement or protected-transaction product so buyers' payments settle with sellers without passing through the platform's own account. Integration, transaction fees and any additional services have their own costs. There is no verified single USD/EUR price for this arrangement; obtain a quote for the countries and payment methods you need.
- In plain language: **In China**, taking buyers' money into the platform's own account and then passing it to sellers can amount to conducting a regulated payment business. Setting up a non-bank payment institution requires approval from the People's Bank of China and at least **CNY 100 million** in paid-up monetary capital. Unauthorized payment activity can be stopped, unlawful proceeds confiscated and fines imposed ranging from hundreds of thousands to millions of yuan. The legal representative and directly responsible people also face fines of **CNY 100,000–500,000**. If the conduct constitutes the criminal offense of illegal business operations, a serious case can result in up to **5 years** in prison; an especially serious case can result in **5 years or more**. For a platform that is not itself authorized to conduct payment business, the practical arrangement is to have the licensed provider settle with sellers and keep those payments out of the platform's own account.
- Benefit: China's Non-Bank Payment Institutions Regulation covers transfers of monetary funds based on electronic payment instructions submitted by the payer or payee. A payment institution must obtain People's Bank of China approval and a payment-business license. The minimum registered capital is **CNY 100 million**, paid up in money. Under article **47**, unauthorized establishment of a non-bank payment institution, or conducting payment business openly or in another form, leads to closure of the activity and confiscation of unlawful proceeds. If those proceeds are at least **CNY 500,000**, the additional fine is **1–5 times** that amount. If there are no unlawful proceeds or they are below **CNY 500,000**, the fine is **CNY 500,000–2 million**. The legal representative or principal person in charge, directly responsible managers and other directly responsible people receive a warning and a separate **CNY 100,000–500,000** fine. Criminal Law article **225(3)** includes unlawful funds-payment and settlement business among illegal business operations. Serious cases carry up to **5 years** of imprisonment or criminal detention; especially serious cases carry **5 years or more** of imprisonment, with the financial penalties specified in that article. The **2019** judicial interpretation sets the ordinary serious-case thresholds at at least **CNY 5 million** in illegal business turnover or **CNY 100,000** in unlawful proceeds. The payment regulation applies nationwide in China from **1 May 2024**.

- Notes: The cited Chinese provisions do not use the informal term *erqing* (二清, often used for a second stage of settlement). The implementing rules instead describe conducting payment business in another form without approval, including transferring monetary funds on users' electronic payment instructions. The judicial interpretation lists cashing out through fictitious transactions, corporate bank accounts and checks, followed by a catch-all category. It does not name platform collection and onward payment as an automatic criminal offense. The recommendation is to keep sellers' funds out of an unauthorized platform's account; this does not replace checking the actual contracts, instructions and fund flow. In specified circumstances, article 3 of the interpretation permits lower criminal thresholds, so the amounts above are not a safe harbor for smaller operations. **In the US**, FinCEN's payment-processor exemption has specific conditions: processing purchases or bills for goods or services other than money transmission, using clearing and settlement systems admitting only financial institutions regulated under the Bank Secrecy Act, a formal agreement, and an agreement at least with the seller or creditor receiving the funds. FinCEN's ruling addresses federal classification; assess any applicable state requirements separately. **In the EU**, PSD2's commercial-agent exclusion concerns an agent authorized to negotiate or conclude sales for only the payer or only the payee. A platform's label does not establish eligibility. **In the UK**, the FCA likewise assesses the business model; a platform providing payment services as a regular business without an applicable exclusion or exemption needs authorization or registration. An escrow arrangement can fall within that scope. Using a payment provider does not by itself settle the platform's own regulatory status.

### 2. In China, classify the service before applying for an ICP license, an EDI license or a filing
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=多 时间=多 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=自由 -->
- Cost: China's non-commercial ICP filing is free. Applying for a value-added telecommunications license requires a legally established company. The minimum registered capital is **CNY 1 million** for operation within a province, autonomous region or directly administered municipality, and **CNY 10 million** for nationwide or cross-region operation. Review takes up to **60 days** after receipt of all application materials; an expert assessment, if required, is excluded from that period. The license is valid for **5 years**. These are Chinese statutory conditions, not USD/EUR prices for starting a website. Local registration, advice, hosting and permit costs depend on the activity and location; no single verified local total is available here.
- In plain language: **In China**, a commercial internet information service needs the appropriate value-added telecommunications license; a non-commercial information service follows the filing route. Commercial information services include supplying information or webpage-production services to internet users for payment. A fee somewhere in the website's business model does not, by itself, resolve the classification of every service it offers. The license applicant must be a company, not an individual. The regional capital thresholds are **CNY 1 million** and **CNY 10 million**, the review period is **60 days**, subject to the conditions above, and the license lasts **5 years**. The filing itself is free. Unauthorized operation or operation beyond the permitted scope can lead to confiscation of unlawful proceeds and a fine of **3–5 times** those proceeds. With no unlawful proceeds, or proceeds below **CNY 50,000**, the fine is **CNY 100,000–1 million**; serious cases can lead to website closure. Online data and transaction processing is a separate telecommunications category: check the actual service rather than treating every online shop as the same licensed business.
- Benefit: China's Internet Information Services Measures distinguish commercial information services, which require a license, from non-commercial services, which require a filing. A service may not operate without the required license or filing. Commercial services include providing information or webpage-production services to internet users for payment. Online data processing and transaction processing covers providing such processing through data or transaction-processing application platforms connected to a public communications network or the internet. Its categories include transaction processing, electronic data interchange and network or electronic-device data processing. For unlicensed operation or operation outside the permitted service scope, unlawful proceeds are confiscated and the fine is **3–5 times** the proceeds; where there are none or they are below **CNY 50,000**, the fine is **CNY 100,000–1 million**. Serious violations of the Internet Information Services Measures can lead to website closure. These are nationwide Chinese rules.
- Evidence grade: A

State Council (**2024**), Decision Amending and Repealing Certain Administrative Regulations, Decree **No. 797**, item **18**. <https://www.gov.cn/gongbao/2025/issue_11786/202501/content_6997034.html>. [EU E-Commerce Directive 2000/31/EC, article 4 and its exceptions](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A02000L0031-20240217).
- Notes: ICP is the internet information-service category; EDI is commonly used for the online data-processing and transaction-processing category. There is no express statement in the cited Chinese provisions that every e-commerce platform combining transaction matching and handling funds must obtain an EDI license. The licensing guidance calls for the license corresponding to the defined business; the service definition is the basis for assessment. Describe what the platform actually does before deciding which license applies. An applicant for the value-added telecommunications license must be a legally established company. A non-commercial filing also does not remove any separate approval required for a regulated service. **In the US**, the SBA directs businesses to determine federal, state and local permits from their activities and location; China's ICP capital thresholds do not apply. **In the EU**, article 4 of the E-Commerce Directive bars prior authorization aimed specifically and exclusively at information-society services, while preserving other authorization schemes and the stated telecommunications exception. This does not exempt a business from payment regulation, general business requirements or rules governing the goods or services it sells. Check the relevant country's requirements; the EU provision is not a rule for every European country.

### 3. In China, obtain the permit for commercial online performances; check whether your company qualifies for an audiovisual-service license
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=多 时间=多 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=自由 -->
- Cost: The provincial cultural authority reviews applications for an Online Culture Business Permit. The rules set a **20-day** decision period after the application is accepted. The permit lasts **3 years**; apply for renewal at least **30 days** before it expires. An applicant for an audiovisual-service license must be a legal entity wholly owned or controlled by the state, among other conditions. These are Chinese eligibility and timing rules. There is no verified USD/EUR total for launching a comparable service in the US or Europe; legal advice, content rights and operating costs depend on the service and country.
- In plain language: **In China**, running a commercial online-performance platform, including services covered by the rules for game demonstrations or commentary, requires an Online Culture Business Permit whose scope expressly includes online performances. The provincial cultural authority has **20 days** to decide after accepting the application, and the permit lasts **3 years**. Keep recordings of all online performances for at least **60 days**. Unauthorized commercial online-culture activity leads to an order to stop, a warning and a fine of up to **CNY 30,000**; refusal to stop leads to inclusion on the cultural-market blacklist. Audiovisual services have a separate permission regime. Its ordinary license-application conditions require a wholly state-owned or state-controlled legal entity, so a private startup generally cannot qualify through that route simply by submitting documents and paying costs.
- Benefit: Online-performance businesses must apply to the provincial cultural authority for an Online Culture Business Permit, with online performances expressly included in its scope. Game demonstrations and commentary are managed by reference to the same measures. Recordings of all online performances must be kept for at least **60 days**. Unauthorized commercial online-culture activity must stop; the authority issues a warning and imposes a fine of up to **CNY 30,000**. Refusal to stop leads to inclusion on the cultural-market blacklist. Internet audiovisual-program services require a License for the Dissemination of Audiovisual Programs through Information Networks, or the filing procedure where the rules provide for it. A license applicant must have legal-person status and be wholly state-owned or state-controlled. Unauthorized audiovisual-service activity leads to a warning and an order to correct the violation; a fine of up to **CNY 30,000** may also be imposed. Serious violations are punished under the Broadcasting and Television Administration Regulation. These are nationwide Chinese rules.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: China's Ministry of Culture (**2016**), Online Performance Business Administration Measures, document **Wenshi [2016] No. 33**, articles **2, 4, 6, 13 and 20**. <https://zwgk.mct.gov.cn/zfxxgkml/scgl/202012/t20201206_918194.html>. Ministry of Culture (**2011**), Interim Provisions on Internet Culture Administration, Order **No. 51**, revised in **2017** by Order **No. 57**, articles **7, 8, 9, 16, 21 and 28**. <https://zwgk.mct.gov.cn/zfxxgkml/zcfg/bmgz/202012/t20201204_905340.html>. State Administration of Radio, Film and Television and Ministry of Information Industry (**2007**), Internet Audiovisual Program Service Administration Provisions, Order **No. 56**, revised in **2015**, articles **2, 7, 8 and 24**. <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/2022-08/23/content_5722664.htm>; [National Radio and Television Administration full text](https://www.nrta.gov.cn/attach/0/9752d6d068e7410caa0e0fe10f78733d.pdf). Cyberspace Administration of China and six other bodies (**2021**), Guiding Opinions on Strengthening the Regulation of Online Livestreaming, document **Guoxinban Fawen [2021] No. 3**, item **9**. <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/2021-02/10/content_5586472.htm>; [Cyberspace Administration publication](https://www.cac.gov.cn/2021-02/09/c_1614442843753738.htm). [US Copyright Office: copyright and the rights of copyright owners](https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/). [European Commission: audiovisual and media services](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/audiovisual-and-media-services).
- Notes: The seven-body document makes the Chinese categories especially clear: commercial online-performance platforms need the culture-business permit and an ICP filing; audiovisual services need the audiovisual license or the applicable registration, plus an ICP filing; internet news-information services need their own news-information-service license. Registration is not a freely interchangeable alternative to every audiovisual license. The performance rules concern commercial online-performance services, not every personal livestream merely because it contains video. When applying the Interim Provisions, use the Ministry of Culture and Tourism's text incorporating the **2017** revision. Even if a page republishes a **2011** act in **2025**, the posting date does not establish which version of the law it contains. **In the US**, check rights to the music, recordings and video you stream: copyright includes distinct rights to reproduce, distribute and publicly perform works, with specific rules for sound recordings. Permission, an applicable statutory exception or another valid basis must cover the actual use. **In the EU**, the Audiovisual Media Services Directive addresses television, on-demand audiovisual services and, under its amended framework, video-sharing platforms. Their rules differ; classify the service before assessing the relevant country's requirements, including protection of minors and commercial communications. A personal stream, an editorially controlled video catalog and a platform hosting users' uploads should not be treated as the same service. The Chinese state-ownership condition is not an EU or US licensing condition.

### 4. In China, complete the required website filing and check the hosting or access provider's telecommunications license
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=中 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=自由 -->
- Cost: China's non-commercial ICP filing is free; the hosting or internet-access provider can submit it on your behalf. Allow days to weeks for the process as a rough planning estimate. The statutory filing period is **20 working days** after the provincial telecommunications authority receives complete materials; checking a provider's license can take about **10 minutes**. Reading public registers costs **$0 or €0**, but servers, bandwidth and any provider assistance can have separate charges. Obtain a quote for those services; the free filing does not establish a free hosting price.
- In plain language: **In China**, arrange the required ICP filing before operating a non-commercial information service through domestic hosting or access. The filing itself is free. Failure to file leads to an order to correct the violation within a deadline and a **CNY 10,000** fine; refusal to correct it leads to website closure. Failure to display the filing number on the homepage carries a separate **CNY 5,000–50,000** fine. Data-center hosting, cloud hosting within the relevant data-center category, and internet-access services are themselves licensed telecommunications businesses. Check the provider's company and permitted service scope in the MIIT register before comparing prices. Moving the server abroad does not by itself establish that the website is exempt from filing or from its other obligations. Where users and transactions remain in China, assess the service's legal scope and the duties covered in entries **5–10**, as well as any additional requirements and costs for transferring data abroad.
- Benefit: Non-commercial internet information services may not operate within China without the required filing. Failure to file leads to an order to correct the violation within a deadline and a **CNY 10,000** fine; refusal to comply leads to website closure. Internet-access providers may not give access to organizations or individuals running such services without a filing. Value-added telecommunications businesses providing access may not supply access or collect payments on behalf of organizations or individuals that lack the legally required business license or non-commercial ICP filing. Data-center services, including the relevant colocation and cloud-hosting services, and internet-access services are licensed value-added telecommunications categories. Failure to display the filing number on the website homepage leads to an order to correct the violation and a **CNY 5,000–50,000** fine. These are nationwide Chinese rules; the filing and homepage-number penalties come from different provisions.

Verify the company and permitted scope before negotiating the price. With an unlicensed or misidentified provider, a failure can leave you struggling to identify the responsible counterparty and obtain redress. Article 5 of the filing measures addresses organizations and individuals within China providing non-commercial internet information services there; it does not define applicability solely by the server's physical location. Treat overseas hosting as a choice requiring a legal assessment, not an automatic way around filing. A move abroad also does not remove any applicable duties in entries **5–10**, and transferring user data abroad can add legal and operating costs. **In the US**, the FTC recommends checking a contractor's data-security practices before outsourcing web hosting or data processing; trace who can access the data, minimize what you retain and plan how to respond to incidents. The provider's contract does not replace the website operator's own assessment of applicable federal, state or sector-specific requirements. **In the EU/EEA**, distinguish the contract governing a processor's work from the safeguards needed for a transfer outside the EEA. The Commission provides separate sets of standard contractual clauses for those purposes; other valid transfer mechanisms also exist. Choosing a server abroad requires checking the actual countries and data flows. These EU safeguards are not China's ICP-filing procedure or a universal requirement to put every European website on a domestic server.

### 5. If other people sell through your platform, verify sellers, submit required information, and retain transaction records
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=多 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=自由 -->
- Cost: Reading the public rules costs USD 0 or EUR 0. Running the platform requires identity and permit checks, registration files, recurring updates, quarterly tax-information reporting, and transaction records retained for at least three years. Staff time, development, and operating the system are ongoing costs. No verified flat estimate in USD/EUR covers every platform; budget for your seller count, transaction volume, and providers' actual fees.
- In plain language: **In China**, check and register each seller's identity, address, contact details, and required administrative permits before admission. Create a file and repeat the checks and updates at least every six months. Send seller information to the market regulator and required tax information to the tax authority quarterly. Keep product, service, and transaction information for at least three years. Failure to meet these duties first brings an order to correct the breach by a deadline; missing that deadline carries a 20,000–100,000 CNY fine. Serious cases can bring suspension for corrective measures and a 100,000–500,000 CNY fine.
- Benefit: Articles 27–28 of China's E-Commerce Law require platforms to obtain, verify, and register sellers' true identity, address, contact information, and administrative permits, create registration files, and check and update them regularly. Article 24 of the online-trading rules makes that interval explicit: at least once every six months. Required seller identity information goes to the market regulator; seller identity and tax-related information go to the tax authority under tax law. Article 31 of the law requires product, service, and transaction information to be retained for at least three years **from completion of the transaction**. Under Article 80, breaching any of those duties brings an order to correct the breach within a specified period; failure to do so carries a 20,000–100,000 CNY fine, while serious cases bring suspension for corrective measures and a 100,000–500,000 CNY fine. Under State Council Order No. 810, required identity information about sellers and workers and their previous-quarter income must be submitted **during the month after the quarter ends**. Late submission falls under the same penalty bands and conditions. These are nationwide Chinese requirements.

- Notes: **In China**, sellers also have business-registration duties unless a statutory exception applies. Article 10 lists individuals selling their own agricultural products or home-made handicrafts, individuals providing convenient household services through their own skills where no permit is required, and occasional small transactions. For the last category, Article 8 of the online-trading rules sets annual turnover at no more than **100,000 CNY**; one seller's turnover across multiple stores on the same or different platforms is combined. This is a registration exception, not a general exemption from taxes or sector-specific permits. Article 27 of the trading rules requires the platform to visibly distinguish registered and unregistered sellers. Article 39 of the law prohibits deleting consumer reviews; Article 15 of the trading rules allows lawful technical measures for prohibited information within a review. It requires collection and verification of specified information, annual seller certification, and certain buyer-facing disclosures; check its coverage, thresholds, and exceptions rather than applying China's six-month check or quarterly tax report. Seller-verification duties are distinct from tax reporting. **EU:** Article 30 of the DSA sets trader-traceability duties for covered marketplaces allowing consumers to conclude distance contracts. Article 29 provides an exception for qualifying micro and small enterprises, except designated very large platforms. Check whether your service qualifies, what information must be verified or displayed, and the separate local tax-reporting rules; neither China's three-year rule nor its fines apply automatically throughout Europe.

### 6. Set up content moderation and reporting; act promptly when you discover prohibited content
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=多 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=自由 -->
- Cost: Moderators and the review system can become your platform's largest ongoing expense. Provide a usable reporting route and records of what was reported, reviewed, and done. No verified universal USD/EUR budget applies: costs depend on posting volume, languages, staffing hours, and the risks to users.
- In plain language: **In China**, the platform is responsible for managing what users post. Set up user registration and account management, publication and comment review, live monitoring, and emergency response. Make the reporting route prominent and easy to use. On discovering unlawful information, stop transmitting it immediately, take the legally required measures to prevent its spread, keep records, and report to the competent authority. Failure to do so brings an order to correct the breach, a warning, and an official notification; a 50,000–500,000 CNY fine **may** also be imposed. Refusal to correct the breach or a serious violation carries a 500,000–2,000,000 CNY fine, with possible suspension, closure of the website or app, and revocation of the relevant permit or business license. Directly responsible managers and other responsible personnel face a separate 50,000–200,000 CNY fine. Plan moderation as an ongoing job that needs staff and resources.
- Benefit: China's content-governance rules make platforms primarily responsible for managing their information content. Required systems cover user registration, account management, publication review, comment review, page and section management, real-time monitoring, emergency response, and handling online rumors and content connected with criminal business networks. Platforms must provide a prominent, convenient complaint and reporting route. When unlawful information is found, they must promptly take lawful measures, preserve records, and report to the competent authority. Article 49 of China's Cybersecurity Law requires an operator to immediately stop transmitting information prohibited by laws or administrative regulations, remove it or take other required measures, prevent its spread, retain records, and report to the competent authority. Under Article 69, failure brings an order to correct the breach, a warning, and an official notification, with a **possible** 50,000–500,000 CNY fine. Refusal to correct the breach or a serious violation carries a 500,000–2,000,000 CNY fine; the authority may also suspend the relevant services or business, close the website or app, or revoke the relevant permit or business license. Directly responsible managers and other responsible personnel are fined 50,000–200,000 CNY. These rules apply nationwide in China.
