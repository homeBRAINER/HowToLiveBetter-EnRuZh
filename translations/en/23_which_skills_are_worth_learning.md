# 23. Which skills are worth learning?

This chapter weighs education and skills mainly against their monetary return and the time they take. Entry 2 also looks at adult mortality. The first six entries help you decide whether to keep studying: legal limits on work at a young age, evidence about health, how common different qualifications are, help when education seems unaffordable, routes onward from vocational school, and a way to do the calculation for yourself. The remaining seven entries concern the choice of a particular skill. Reliable official Chinese earnings figures for each occupation are difficult to verify, so this chapter gives no promised monthly wage for a specific trade. It concentrates on evidence about returns to education, ways to check whether a certificate or training subsidy is legitimate, and features that may make a skill harder to replace.

The introduction and all 13 entries are translated. The [Chinese chapter](../../book/23-学什么技能划算.md) is available for comparison.

### 1. Before taking a job as a teenager, check the age rules and get your employment documented
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=自由 -->
- Cost: Checking your age and the applicable rules costs $0 or €0 and takes a little time. This is not the cost of a job or of education.
- In plain language: Someone leaving lower secondary school may be 15 or 16. In China, employers generally cannot hire anyone under 16. An employer who does so faces penalties, while undocumented work may make it harder for the young worker to prove what happened and claim protection. Do not accept a job with unclear terms or no record of the working relationship. Lawful light work may be possible at 14 in Russia and Belarus; the US and EU have their own age rules. Check the law where you live before choosing work over further study.
- Benefit: China's Compulsory Education Law requires a parent or other guardian to send a child to school at age **six** and ensure that the child completes compulsory education (Article 11). It prohibits hiring a child who should still be receiving compulsory education (Article 14), and provides for instruction and an order to correct an unjustified failure to send the child to school (Article 58). China's Provisions on the Prohibition of Child Labour ban government bodies, associations, enterprises, institutions, private non-enterprise organizations and individual businesses from hiring children **under 16** (Article 2). The employer is fined **5,000 yuan per child per month**. If it does not return the child to a parent or guardian after an order within the prescribed time, the rate becomes **10,000 yuan per child per month** (Article 6). Introducing a child under 16 to an employer brings a **5,000-yuan fine per child** (Article 7). If an illegally employed child is sick or injured, the employer must arrange treatment and pay all medical and living costs during it; disability or death also calls for a lump-sum payment calculated under occupational-injury insurance rules (Article 10). Professional arts and sports organizations may employ under-16 performers and athletes with guardian consent while protecting their health and schooling. Regulated, safe educational practice and vocational training are not classed as child labour (Article 13).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Standing Committee of the National People's Congress of China (2018), Compulsory Education Law, second 2018 amendment, Articles 11, 14 and 58, published by the Ministry of Education. <http://www.moe.gov.cn/jyb_sjzl/sjzl_zcfg/zcfg_jyfl/202110/t20211029_575949.html>. State Council of China (2002), Provisions on the Prohibition of Child Labour, Decree 364, Articles 2, 6, 7, 10 and 13. <http://www.gov.cn/gongbao/content/2002/content_61798.htm>; [current Ministry of Justice text](https://xzfg.moj.gov.cn/front/law/detail?LawID=604). US Department of Labor, [child-labor rules for non-agricultural jobs](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/43-child-labor-non-agriculture). European Union, [Directive 94/33/EC on the protection of young people at work](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/1994/33/oj/eng). Russian Labour Ministry, [guidance on employment contracts](https://mintrud.gov.ru/ministry/programms/trudotn/dogovor). Belarusian Tax Ministry, [explanation of Labour Code Article 21](https://nalog.gov.by/clarifications/clarifications/13891/).
- Notes: The fines in yuan are **Chinese statutory amounts**, not local costs or penalties in the US or Europe. The Chinese ban must not be presented as a universal ban on all under-16 work. Under US federal rules for *covered non-agricultural jobs*, 14- and 15-year-olds may work outside school in specified nonhazardous jobs with limits on hours; 16- and 17-year-olds may work longer hours but remain barred from federally hazardous occupations. State law can be stricter. In the EU, the baseline minimum age is generally tied to the end of compulsory full-time schooling and at least 15, with nationally defined exceptions for light work, training, and cultural or artistic activity; European countries outside the EU have their own laws. Russia generally allows employment at 16 and, with conditions, light work at 15 or 14; Belarus generally allows it at 16 and certain light work at 14 with a parent's or guardian's written consent. An unlawfully employed child in China is **not** without legal protection: Article 10 expressly obliges the employer to cover illness and injury costs. Records of work, agreements, and pay can matter when claiming rights; see [chapter 19](19_work_exit_and_injury.md). Reaching 16 does not guarantee safe or well-paid work; entry 10 considers how easily routine tasks can be replaced.

### 2. Consider the link between education and longevity without treating 1.9% as a personal promise
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=多 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: More years of education take time and effort. Tuition, if any, and available aid depend on the country, program, and your circumstances; entry 4 describes Chinese support measures. There is no one honest USD or EUR price for everyone.
- In plain language: Across the studies, adults with more years of education tended to live longer. A review of **603 studies in 59 countries** associated each extra year of education with about **1.9% lower relative risk of death** in adulthood. The estimate was **2.9%** at ages 18–49 and **0.8%** at age 70 or older. Education may affect health and income, but family resources, where you live, and working conditions can also matter. The association belongs in the decision; it cannot predict your personal lifespan.
- Benefit: A systematic review and meta-analysis searched publications from **January 1980 to 31 May 2023**, with the search updated on **16 June 2023**. It included **603 studies**, **70 locations in 59 countries**, and **10,355 observations**. Years of education had a dose–response association with all-cause adult mortality: each additional year was associated on average with **1.9% lower relative risk** (**95% uncertainty interval: 1.8–2.0%**). The age-specific estimates were **2.9%** (**2.8–3.0%**) for adults aged **18–49** and **0.8%** (**0.6–1.0%**) for those aged **70 or older**. The model found no differential association by sex or country Socio-demographic Index level. These are not percentage-point changes in a person's absolute chance of death.
- Evidence grade: A (disputed)
- Sources: IHME-CHAIN Collaborators (2024), “Effects of education on adult mortality: a global systematic review and meta-analysis”, *The Lancet Public Health*, 9(3), e155–e165. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(23)00306-7>; [PubMed abstract and reference](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38278172/); [full article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10901745/).
- Notes: The included evidence was observational. The authors reported evidence of publication bias (**p < 0.0001**) and substantial variation between studies; **85.9%** of the observations came from high-income countries. Statistical adjustment cannot fully separate schooling from family resources, prior health, place of residence, and occupation. The group-level association between more education and lower mortality is strong, but it does **not** prove that your next year in school will *cause* your own risk to fall by 1.9%. You are the potential beneficiary; calculate the financial side separately.

### 3. Before calling education worthless, look at how educational levels are distributed
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Looking up the published figures costs $0 or €0 and takes a little time.
- In plain language: China's **2020** census counted **15,467 people per 100,000** at the college or university education level (about 15%), **15,088** at senior secondary level, including vocational secondary education, **34,507** at junior secondary level, and **24,767** at primary level. Among people aged 15 or older, estimated average schooling was **9.91 years**, just over the length of junior secondary schooling in China. These figures show how widespread each level of education was across China's population; college or university education accounted for a smaller share. Stories about a graduate of a **Project 985** university delivering food or a junior-secondary leaver owning a business cannot tell you how often either outcome occurs. Compare yourself with people your own age and in your intended field before drawing conclusions.
- Benefit: In China's seventh population census, whose reference date was **1 November 2020**, **218,360,767 people** were counted at college or university education level, **213,005,258** at senior secondary level, including vocational secondary school, **487,163,489** at junior secondary level, and **349,658,828** at primary level. Between **2010** and **2020**, the counts per **100,000** inhabitants changed as follows: college or university, **8,930 to 15,467**; senior secondary, **14,032 to 15,088**; junior secondary, **38,788 to 34,507**; and primary, **26,779 to 24,767**. For people **aged 15 or older**, estimated average schooling rose from **9.08 to 9.91 years**, while the illiteracy rate fell from **4.08% to 2.67%**.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: National Bureau of Statistics of China and Office of the Leading Group of the State Council for the Seventh National Population Census (2021), *Communiqué of the Seventh National Population Census, No. 6: Education of the population*. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/tjgb/rkpcgb/qgrkpcgb/202302/t20230206_1902006.html>.
- Notes: The denominator is the **whole population of mainland China's 31 provincial-level areas plus active military personnel**, not its workforce or job applicants. It excludes residents of Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan living in mainland China and foreign nationals. Each education category includes **graduates, current students, and people who left before graduating**; thus 15,467 per 100,000 is **not** the proportion holding a completed degree. Average years of schooling is calculated from education-level categories, rather than each person's exact time in school. The share with college or university experience can be higher among young people than across all ages, so the national figure cannot establish your standing among peers. Neither can it show that a junior-secondary leaver is easier to replace at work. It does not answer whether a specific course will pay off; use the calculation in entry 6. These Chinese figures must not be treated as US or European statistics. The comparison is for your own decision.

### 4. Before giving up on education because of cost, calculate the aid you can actually receive
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Asking a school about support and checking eligibility costs $0 or €0, although preparing an application takes time. Tuition, housing, and living expenses may still be substantial.
- In plain language: Compulsory education in China is free of tuition and standard school fees. Since **2012**, specified groups of students in public secondary vocational schools have had tuition waived. The national *average* annual grant for eligible vocational secondary and general senior-secondary students increased from **2,000 to 2,300 yuan** starting in the spring **2025** term. For college and university students, the average grant rose from **3,300 to 3,700 yuan a year**, and the National Encouragement Scholarship from **5,000 to 6,000 yuan** in **2024**. Annual maximum national student-loan applications rose from **16,000 to 20,000 yuan** for undergraduates and from **20,000 to 25,000 yuan** for graduate students, with interest at the corresponding loan prime rate (LPR) minus **70 basis points**. These are **Chinese** programs and amounts, not US or European aid. Find out which grants, fee waivers, funded places, or loans you personally qualify for. Then compare the remaining cost of different courses, including earnings you would give up while studying. You may need to delay or change your route; aid is not automatic.
- Benefit: From the autumn **2012** term, tuition waivers in Chinese public secondary vocational schools covered full-time students in **years 1, 2 and 3** from rural areas, including county towns, students of urban agriculture-related programs, and students from low-income families. Related performing-arts programs were excluded. A **2024** policy raised the average national college and undergraduate grant from **3,300 to 3,700 yuan per student per year** from the autumn **2024** term, and the National Encouragement Scholarship from **5,000 to 6,000 yuan** in **2024**. The average national grant for vocational secondary and general senior-secondary education rose from **2,000 to 2,300 yuan per student per year** from the spring **2025** term. From autumn **2024**, the annual ceiling on national student-loan applications rose from **16,000 to 20,000 yuan** for college and undergraduate students and from **20,000 to 25,000 yuan** for graduate students; the interest rate became the matching-term LPR minus **70 basis points**. Neither a loan ceiling nor an average grant guarantees that amount to any one student.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: China's Ministry of Finance, National Development and Reform Commission, Ministry of Education, and Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security (2012), **财教〔2012〕376号**. <http://www.gov.cn/gongbao/content/2013/content_2332779.htm>; [Ministry of Education publication](https://www.moe.gov.cn/jyb_xxgk/moe_1777/moe_1779/201210/t20121030_143848.html). China's Ministry of Finance, Ministry of Education, and Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security (2024), explanation of student-aid changes. <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/202410/content_6983672.htm>; [Ministry of Education policy notice](https://www.moe.gov.cn/jyb_xxgk/moe_1777/moe_1779/202410/t20241029_1159726.html). China's Ministry of Finance, Ministry of Education, People's Bank, and financial regulator (2024), **财教〔2024〕188号**. <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202411/content_6984812.htm>; [Ministry of Education publication](https://www.moe.gov.cn/jyb_xxgk/moe_1777/moe_1779/202411/t20241104_1160686.html). [US Federal Student Aid: free FAFSA application](https://studentaid.gov/articles/fafsa-student-steps/) and [how to compare aid offers](https://studentaid.gov/articles/evaluating-financial-aid-offers/). [European Commission: country profiles for studying in Europe](https://education.ec.europa.eu/eu/study-in-europe/countries).
- Notes: The possible beneficiaries are you and your children. In China, the exact coverage of waivers and grant tiers depends on province and school. The National Encouragement Scholarship is for academically strong students in financial need; [China's Ministry of Education sets out the eligibility rules](https://www.moe.gov.cn/jyb_xxgk/moe_1777/moe_1779/201410/t20141021_178435.html). A private vocational school's subsidy may cover only the local public-school standard; the student pays any difference. **Loans must be repaid**: check the interest rate, repayment schedule, and total repayment. In the US, submit the free **FAFSA** by the relevant deadlines to be considered for federal, state, and school grants, work-study, and loans; compare the actual offers and remember that a work-study job is not guaranteed. In Europe, fees and aid depend on the **country, institution, course, and student status**; consult the country's current rules and the school's aid office, not a single “European” rate. Record tuition, housing, travel, foregone earnings, and confirmed aid for each course. If household income has stopped, also see [chapter 7](07_when_money_is_tight.md) for support and training while unemployed. The yuan amounts describe Chinese policies and are not converted into dollars or euros.

### 5. If general upper-secondary admission does not work out, check the vocational route onward
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Checking admission rules and course plans costs $0 or €0; do it before submitting applications.
- In plain language: Missing a place in general upper-secondary school need not end your education at junior-secondary level. China's **2022** Vocational Education Law gives vocational education equal standing in importance with general education and provides routes from secondary to higher vocational education. Higher vocational colleges may consider both academic knowledge and practical skills. Vocational-school graduates have dedicated admission routes, and employers may not obstruct fair competition for jobs. In the US, a community college can offer career training and a route toward a bachelor's degree, but admission and credit transfer depend on the institutions. Across Europe, vocational routes and access to further study differ by country and program. Check the next step offered by the specific course you are considering.
- Benefit: China's Vocational Education Law, revised in **2022** and effective **1 May 2022**, recognizes vocational and general education as equally important types in **Article 3**. **Article 37** allows linked admission and training from secondary to higher vocational education in relevant programs; higher vocational schools may admit students through assessments combining academic knowledge with vocational skills. Education authorities at **provincial level and above** must operate a common platform listing schools, courses, and admissions information. **Article 53** gives vocational-school students equal opportunities in further education, work, and career development compared with general-school students at the same level. Higher vocational schools and general universities offering vocational education must reserve a proportion of their admission places or arrange separate examinations for vocational-school graduates. Employers may not set hiring or application conditions that obstruct those graduates' fair competition. For public-institution recruitment to jobs with vocational skill-level requirements, educational-level requirements may be appropriately lowered.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Standing Committee of the National People's Congress of China (2022), *Vocational Education Law*, 2022 revision, Articles 3, 37, and 53, [published by the Ministry of Education](http://www.moe.gov.cn/jyb_sjzl/sjzl_zcfg/zcfg_jyfl/202204/t20220421_620064.html). [US Department of Education: community college routes to work and transfer](https://www.ed.gov/higher-education/find-college-or-educational-program/community-college) and [accreditation lookup](https://www.ed.gov/about/contact-us/faqs). [European Commission: vocational education and training varies across countries](https://education.ec.europa.eu/education-levels/vocational-education-and-training/about-vocational-education-and-training).
- Notes: You and your children may benefit. Chinese law creates **routes**, not guaranteed admission or employment: linked programs, reserved places, and exam rules vary by province and school. In the US, ask the receiving college which credits it will accept before relying on a transfer route; use the federal accreditation database to check a postsecondary provider's status. In Europe, check the national education authority's current admission rules and the institution's published course plan rather than assuming one route works everywhere. Check the provider, course, duration, price, and eligibility before enrolling. Entry 10 offers a way to examine how much hands-on work and on-site judgment a skill requires, without promising protection from automation. Entry 8 explains how to verify a **professional certificate**; those checks do not replace checking the school itself. Treat “guaranteed job” or “guaranteed admission” claims as unproven unless the exact conditions can be verified.

### 6. Work out what pays off for you: earning now or continuing your education
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: A calculation on paper or in a spreadsheet costs $0 or €0 and takes about half an hour. The course itself may cost money; a loan changes when you pay, not whether the debt is owed.
- In plain language: Put both routes in the same currency: dollars in the US or euros where that is the local currency. Working now brings pay over the next **three years**, but your later annual earnings might be lower. Studying has tuition, books, travel, and any extra living costs; subtract confirmed fee waivers and grants that do not have to be repaid. Add the pay you would forgo during those years. **A loan is not a discount**: it helps cover the bill now but has to be repaid, with interest. Compare plausible differences in annual take-home pay over the remaining **30–40 years** of work, taking into account employment chances and the lower value of money received far in the future. Consider the observational education-and-health association in entry 2 separately; it is no promise of a longer life. Early pay arrives once, whereas a persistent annual earnings gap accumulates, so the answer depends on the course, its cost, and actual jobs.
- Benefit: A study of **urban Chinese earnings from 1988 to 2001** estimated that the association between one additional year of schooling and annual earnings rose from **4.0% in 1988** to **10.2% in 2001**. Most of the increase occurred **after 1992** and reflected a rising wage premium for higher education. This is a historical point of reference for thinking about annual earnings, not a rate to insert into anyone's current personal budget. Entry 7 discusses a global average.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Zhang J, Zhao Y, Park A, Song X (2005), “Economic returns to schooling in urban China, 1988 to 2001”, *Journal of Comparative Economics*, 33(4), 730–752. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jce.2005.05.008>; [publisher's article page](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S014759670500048X).
- Notes: The study covers **urban China through 2001**. Its **4.0% and 10.2%** estimates cannot be applied to the US, Europe, or a particular person today. The **9%** global average in entry 7 is no personal guarantee either. This is a method for asking the question, not the answer: an expensive course with a poor match to actual jobs might fail to pay for itself, without proving that all education lacks value. Compare earnings **after tax** under several realistic job scenarios. Do not count borrowed money as a grant. If tuition is already counted, do not add the same loan principal to tuition a second time; track interest and the repayment schedule separately. Beyond pay, qualifications can open jobs with formal entry requirements (entry 5), make a career change possible, and develop skills that may be harder to replace (entry 10). The personal benefit depends on your route.

### 7. Use returns to schooling as a reference, not a promise of 9% from any course
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Comparing options costs $0 or €0 and a little time. The training itself may have a substantial price.
- In plain language: Across **1,120 estimates from 139 countries**, an additional year of schooling was associated with about **9% higher earnings on average**. Estimates for women remained higher than those for men. This concerns **a year of education**, not the return from signing up for a short course. If you are considering a **six-month course**, write down its full quoted price in dollars or euros, extra expenses, and the earnings you would forgo. Then check which jobs the skill actually opens up and what they pay. Compare plausible earnings differences with the costs using the method in entry 6; do not automatically add 9% to your own pay.
- Benefit: The review spans **1950–2014**, **139 countries**, and **1,120 estimates**. Its authors round the global average *private return to an additional year of schooling* to about **9%**. The main Mincerian analysis, based on **705 estimates**, finds **8.8%**. Estimated private returns to higher education had risen; social returns to education remained high, and average estimated returns for women were higher than for men. A social return is not an individual's pay increase.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Psacharopoulos G, Patrinos HA (2018), “Returns to investment in education: a decennial review of the global literature”, *Education Economics*, 26(5), 445–458. <https://doi.org/10.1080/09645292.2018.1484426>; [full paper hosted by the World Bank](https://datatopics.worldbank.org/education/files/GlobalAchievement/ReturnsInteractive.pdf). World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 8402. <https://doi.org/10.1596/1813-9450-8402>.
- Notes: This is an average for **an extra year of education**, not a study of commercial course payoffs or a current US or European rate. Its data end in **2014**; countries, fields, course lengths, quality, and labour markets differ greatly. The paper also distinguishes earnings-function estimates from fully discounted investment returns; their percentages cannot simply be compared as identical yields. For a specific six-month course, use the **confirmed price in USD or EUR**, not a universal figure or a currency conversion from another country. Check the chance of getting a different job and its actual pay. A course can have a negative return. The potential beneficiary is you.

### 8. Before paying for a certificate, check what it proves and who issues it
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Checking public directories and asking the issuer usually costs $0 or €0. Allow roughly **10–20 minutes** for an initial check.
- In plain language: An impressive certificate title does not grant permission to practise a regulated occupation. Before paying, answer three questions: is the promised document a government qualification, an independently assessed skill credential, or simply proof of course completion; does the issuer have the authority it claims; and can the particular credential be verified through the relevant issuer or official service? China's labour ministry has investigated misleading training offers, unjustified fees, mass issuance of dubious certificates, and organisations impersonating authorities. In the US, check whether the job requires a **state licence** and who issues it; a voluntary industry certification is a different thing. In an EU country, check whether the profession is regulated **there** and which authority decides whether your qualification is accepted.
- Benefit: In **2022**, China's Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security launched a nationwide campaign after organisations advertised training, assessments, and certificates in the names of new occupations or skill grades without proper authority, charged unwarranted fees, issued certificates indiscriminately, overstated their value, or impersonated official bodies. The notice directed officials to investigate misuse of state names and symbols, misleading publicity, training beyond an organisation's permission, improper fees, and forged credentials. It called for work with internet regulators, police, and market regulators, including action against unlawful online and social-media advertising, and for public information on checking issuers and certificates. Verifying the claimed status before paying can prevent a mistaken purchase; the notice does not quantify money saved.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: China's Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security (2022), notice on a special campaign against counterfeit technical and vocational certificates. <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/2022-03/18/content_5679641.htm>; [ministry publication](https://chinajob.mohrss.gov.cn/c/2022-05-10/349846.shtml). [China's official skills-evaluation portal](https://osta.mohrss.gov.cn/) and [government instructions for checking certificate records](https://app.www.gov.cn/govdata/gov/202201/06/480434/article.html). [US Department of Labor's CareerOneStop training and credential tools](https://www.careeronestop.org/FindTraining/find-training.aspx); [its explanation of licences versus certifications](https://cloudfront.careeronestop.org/toolkit/Training/find-licenses-help.aspx). [European Commission database of regulated professions](https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/single-market/services/free-movement-professionals/database-regulated-professions_en) and [Your Europe guidance](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/work/professional-qualifications/regulated-professions/).
- Notes: **Do not mix up China's categories.** If an occupation is absent from the current national professional-qualifications catalogue, a document cannot truthfully claim to be *that* state qualification. A separate skill-grade certificate may still be valid: check the registered evaluator, its authorised scope, and the corresponding official certificate platform. If a claimed official credential is not yet found, ask the issuer and responsible authority why; do not pay for its claimed official status until it can be confirmed. A private course certificate can be genuine without being a legal licence or state qualification. In the US, state licensing requirements vary; a voluntary certification's value depends on the issuer and employers. Across Europe, regulation and recognition depend on the country and profession. If the EU database has no match, ask the national contact point rather than assuming no rules apply. Words such as “international”, “senior”, or “registered” call for checking the specific issuer; alone they prove neither value nor fraud. “Guaranteed pass” or promises of passive income from lending out a credential are strong warning signs. Chinese registers cannot be used as US or European work authorisations.

### 9. Check subsidised training before paying for a commercial course
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: If you qualify for a fully funded programme, tuition may be $0 or €0; checking the conditions and taking the course still take time. Other schemes cover only part of the price, so find out your own share before enrolling.
- In plain language: Check public employment-service programmes and ask your employer about training before paying a private provider. China plans more than **30 million subsidised training attendances** between **2025 and the end of 2027**; these are course attendances, not necessarily distinct people. Several funding streams are involved, and subsidy rates depend partly on how scarce a skill is and how much training costs. In the US, ask your local **American Job Center** whether you qualify for Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA) training and which programmes are approved. In Europe, check your country's or region's employment service and training schemes. Approval of a course alone does not mean that you personally qualify for funding.
- Benefit: China's **2025–2027** plan calls for more than **30 million subsidised training attendances** in priority industries, occupations with unmet demand, and priority jobseeker groups. Funding sources include employment-support funds, unemployment insurance, industry-development funds, interregional cooperation funds, and employer education budgets. Subsidies are to vary with both occupational shortages **and training costs**; some market-priced courses may combine public support with a learner's payment. Employers are instructed to spend **at least 60% of their employee-education funds** on frontline workers' education and training. This is a rule for employer budgets in China, not a promise that each worker gets 60% of any course fee paid.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: China's State Council Leading Group for Employment Promotion and Labour Protection (2025), guidance on large-scale vocational skills training, [official publication](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202507/content_7032222.htm) and [government text](https://app.www.gov.cn/govdata/gov/202507/15/532869/article.html). [US Department of Labor's CareerOneStop: WIOA-eligible training programmes and individual eligibility](https://www.careeronestop.org/LocalHelp/EmploymentAndTraining/find-WIOA-training-programs.aspx). [European Social Fund Plus: national and regional programme contacts](https://european-social-fund-plus.ec.europa.eu/en/in-your-country).
- Notes: China's **30 million attendances** and **60% employer-budget rule** create no US or European entitlement. Chinese provinces specify their own course lists, eligibility and payment levels: check the local human-resources authority's subsidy catalogue and public practical-training centres; employed workers can also ask about their employer's training budget. [Chapter 7](07_when_money_is_tight.md) describes a Chinese limit of up to **3** subsidised training awards per person under the scheme cited there; check current local terms before counting on another award. In the US, a programme's appearance on a WIOA-eligible provider list does **not** establish that an applicant qualifies for financial help; ask an American Job Center about individual eligibility and actual funding. In Europe, ESF+ programmes and the way to apply vary by country and region. Confirm the specific course, your eligibility, and any remaining fee before committing.

### 10. Examine hands-on, unpredictable, and social tasks when choosing a skill
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Comparing several occupations' real tasks costs $0 or €0 and a little time; training and equipment may cost money.
- In plain language: A model covering **702 US occupations** put roughly **47% of US employment** in occupations at high *potential* risk of computerisation. Its authors identified three hard-to-automate task groups: physical perception and manipulation in difficult settings, creative problem-solving, and social interaction. Look for these tasks in actual job descriptions and practice; do not buy a course because someone calls the occupation “automation-proof”. On-site installation, repairs, and care work can involve such tasks, but technology can still change parts of those jobs. Routine information-processing tasks are generally easier to standardise than work in unpredictable surroundings.
- Benefit: Frey and Osborne estimated the computerisation potential of **702 US occupations**, assigning occupations representing about **47% of US employment** to a high-risk category. Their three engineering bottlenecks were **perception and manipulation** (including finger and manual dexterity, cramped workspaces and awkward positions), **creative intelligence**, and **social intelligence**. A later OECD analysis of *individual jobs' tasks* rather than whole occupations estimated an average of about **9%** potentially automatable jobs across **21 OECD countries**. The different methods and populations do not measure actual job losses.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Frey CB, Osborne MA (2017), “The future of employment: how susceptible are jobs to computerisation?”, *Technological Forecasting and Social Change*, 114, 254–280. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.techfore.2016.08.019>. Arntz M, Gregory T, Zierahn U (2016), [“The Risk of Automation for Jobs in OECD Countries”](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/the-risk-of-automation-for-jobs-in-oecd-countries_5jlz9h56dvq7-en.html), OECD Social, Employment and Migration Working Papers 189. [OECD explanation of occupation-based and task-based estimates](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/is-education-losing-the-race-with-technology_73105f99-en/full-report/component-4.html).
- Notes: Frey and Osborne's study began as a **2013** working paper and appeared in a journal in **2017**. The **47%** is a modelled share of US employment in high-risk occupations, not a claim that 47% of workers have lost or will lose their jobs by a certain date. The OECD's **9%** across **21 countries** is also not a forecast of layoffs or a local rate for every country. Artificial intelligence and robotics change what machines can do. Compare the tasks, working conditions, local demand, and chances to keep learning instead of relying on any one percentage.

### 11. Check who recognises a skill level and whether it affects pay
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Checking an occupation's requirements and asking an employer about pay costs $0 or €0; an assessment itself may have a fee.
- In plain language: Before paying for a course, find out what qualification it leads to, who recognises it, and whether an employer pays more for it. China is promoting a **“new eight-level skilled-worker”** system and advising employers to link pay both to the value of a role and to the recognised skill level. That is a policy direction, not a guaranteed raise. In the US, licensing and voluntary certification differ by occupation and state. Across Europe, formal entry requirements and recognition vary by country and profession. A skill without an official ladder can still earn more; a certificate with one does not automatically raise your wage.
- Benefit: China's **2025** guidance promotes its **new eight-level** skill-grade system, more assessment and appointment at senior-worker levels, and employer pay systems linked to both job value and skill grade. Qualifying employers may assess their own workers, and outstanding workers can be assigned a grade without meeting the normal requirements for years of service, a preceding grade, or other qualifications. A **2021** “Skills China” plan laid earlier groundwork. Neither document gives a universal pay increase for earning a certificate.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: China's State Council Leading Group for Employment Promotion and Labour Protection (2025), [vocational-training guidance](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202507/content_7032222.htm), sections 14 and 16. China's Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security (2021), [“Skills China” implementation plan](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/2021-07/06/content_5622619.htm). For local distinctions, see the [US Department of Labor's guidance on licences and certifications](https://cloudfront.careeronestop.org/toolkit/Training/find-licenses-help.aspx) and the [European Commission's regulated-professions database](https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/single-market/services/free-movement-professionals/database-regulated-professions_en).
- Notes: China's **eight-level** framework is not a US or Europe-wide pay scale. Formal entry rules, qualification checks and pay agreements depend on the place and occupation; see [entry 8](#8-before-paying-for-a-certificate-check-what-it-proves-and-who-issues-it). Ask specific employers whether they recognise the credential and what, if anything, it changes in the offer or contract. Without a formal grade, better skills or a different role can still improve earnings. With a grade, a raise is not automatic.

### 12. Check local demand instead of trusting a headline salary
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Looking through official training lists, vacancies and working conditions costs $0 or €0; talking with workers and employers takes time.
- In plain language: Find the local list of occupations in demand and eligible training, then compare real vacancies and ask people doing the work about conditions. A larger subsidy does **not** by itself prove a bigger labour shortage: China's policy considers both shortage and training cost. A story about high pay also leaves out hours, travel, exposure to hazards and seasonal gaps in work.
- Benefit: China's guidance calls for differentiated subsidies based on **occupational shortage and training cost**; regions select training subjects in light of local industry and skill needs. A subsidised-course list can help start a demand check, but **differences in subsidy amounts between cities cannot be read directly as differences in shortage**. Checking vacancies and speaking with employers can test the opportunity; the policy does not promise a wage or a job.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: China's State Council Leading Group for Employment Promotion and Labour Protection (2025), [vocational-training guidance](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202507/content_7032222.htm), sections 17–18. [US Department of Labor's American Job Center finder and services](https://cloudfront.careeronestop.org/LocalHelp/AmericanJobCenters/american-job-centers.aspx). [European Social Fund Plus national and regional programme contacts](https://european-social-fund-plus.ec.europa.eu/en/in-your-country).
- Notes: There is no verified, single official table here giving comparable **average wages and shortage rankings for every occupation nationwide**. Do not replace it with selected success stories, a recruitment site's advertised rate, or one person's experience. In China, check the local human-resources authority's training catalogue and public employment service. In the US, ask the local American Job Center and compare actual job postings. In Europe, use the relevant national or regional employment service. Compare hours, commute, safety, seasonal work, and real offers after training. A posted wage is an employer's offer, not a proven occupational average.

### 13. Prefer training with a clear route into work when a short course is sufficient
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Record the specific course's confirmed price in USD or EUR, materials, travel, duration and foregone earnings. A short course need not be cheap; a suitable programme from entry 9 may reduce or remove the fee.
- In plain language: A short programme needs a testable goal: it should qualify you to apply for a particular job or teach a defined part of it. China's policy encourages short courses that lead quickly towards employment and “micro-majors” in vocational colleges. Length alone does not prove quality. Some occupations require longer study, supervised practice, or a legal licence. Compare the course with actual job requirements, and decide in advance when to stop spending time and money if the work does not materialise.
- Benefit: China's **2025** guidance supports high-quality, market-priced private training projects that can lead to employment in a short period, and encourages vocational colleges to offer **micro-majors** and vocational-capability courses focused on skills employers urgently need. This supports a training format; it does not measure any course's return or guarantee a job after a short programme.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: China's State Council Leading Group for Employment Promotion and Labour Protection (2025), [vocational-training guidance](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202507/content_7032222.htm), section 11.
- Notes: Ask for the syllabus, practical exercises, exact total price and the document awarded. Compare them with several vacancies and, for a regulated occupation, the mandatory entry rules. A **six-month or one-year** programme can be worthwhile if that is what genuine competence takes; include foregone earnings in its cost. Set an exit condition before starting — for example, the stage and number of unsuccessful applications after which you will reconsider the choice; see [chapter 4](04_use_your_time_well.md). Chinese policy and course duration guarantee no job in the US or Europe.
