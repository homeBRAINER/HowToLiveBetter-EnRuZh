# 2. Don’t damage your health over time

This chapter focuses on chronic risk factors with substantial effects on all-cause mortality and strong supporting evidence, ordered by benefit relative to cost. Except for the randomized trials identified below, the figures come from observational research. Confounding and reverse causation can affect the hazard ratios (HRs) and risk ratios (RRs). Read them as estimates of the direction and size of an association, not as a promised reduction in your own risk.

### 1. Quit smoking—the sooner, the better
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Getting through withdrawal takes effort over weeks or months. You stop spending money on cigarettes: if you smoke a pack a day, your monthly saving before treatment costs is roughly 30 times the price you actually pay per pack, in USD or EUR. Medication or counseling may have a separate cost; quitting does not require doing it without support.
- In plain language: In a large US study, people who smoked had a life expectancy more than ten years shorter than those who had never smoked. Quitting before 40 avoided about 90% of the extra risk associated with continuing to smoke. That is a reduction in the excess risk from smoking, not a 90% reduction in every cause of death. Quitting earlier was associated with more years of life regained.
- Benefit: In an observational US cohort, current smokers had a life expectancy more than 10 years shorter than never-smokers. Quitting before age 40 avoided about 90% of the excess mortality risk associated with continued smoking. People who quit at ages 25–34, 35–44 and 45–54 gained about 10, 9 and 6 years of life, respectively, compared with those who continued. In observational Chinese cohorts, the study centered around 2010 found an all-cause mortality RR of 1.65 for urban male smokers and 1.22 for rural male smokers. Among people who stopped by choice, rather than because of illness, little excess risk remained after 10 years of cessation.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Jha P et al. (2013). 21st-century hazards of smoking and benefits of cessation in the United States. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMsa1211128> ; Chen Z et al. (2015). Contrasting male and female trends in tobacco-attributed mortality in China: evidence from successive nationwide prospective cohort studies. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(15)00340-2>
- Notes: Secondhand smoke also kills. An analysis of 192 countries estimated that exposure caused about 603,000 deaths worldwide in 2004, approximately 1% of all deaths that year (Öberg M et al., 2011. Worldwide burden of disease from exposure to second-hand smoke: a retrospective analysis of data from 192 countries. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(10)61388-8>). Avoid other people’s smoke even if you do not smoke yourself, and keep children away from it.

> Editorial clarification: These are population estimates, not a deadline before which smoking is safe. The 2004 secondhand-smoke estimate is historical, not a current annual count. “Little excess risk after ten years” describes the Chinese study group; it does not mean that every disease risk returns to a never-smoker’s level on that schedule.

### 2. Keep your home and car smoke-free, including when guests visit
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No spending. The difficult part can be asking relatives and guests not to smoke indoors.
- In plain language: Secondhand smoke was estimated to cause about 600,000 deaths worldwide in 2004; nearly three in ten were children. A meta-analysis found higher odds of high blood pressure, heart disease, heart attack and stroke among people exposed to it, with estimates roughly 30%–50% higher depending on the outcome. The association with cardiovascular disease was stronger for exposure at home than elsewhere.
- Benefit: In 2004, an estimated 603,000 deaths worldwide were attributable to secondhand smoke, about 1.0% of all deaths; 28% were children. A meta-analysis of 57 studies reported odds ratios of 1.28 for hypertension, 1.39 for heart disease, 1.50 for myocardial infarction and 1.36 for stroke, with a stronger association for exposure at home than outside the home.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Öberg M, Jaakkola MS, Woodward A, Peruga A, Prüss-Ustün A (2011). Worldwide burden of disease from exposure to second-hand smoke: a retrospective analysis of data from 192 countries. Lancet. The estimated 603,000 deaths in 2004 represented 1.0% of global mortality; 47% were women, 28% children and 26% men, with rounding. Children accounted for 61% of disability-adjusted life years lost. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(10)61388-8> ; Feng S et al. (2026). The Associations Between Secondhand Smoke Exposure and Various Cardiovascular Diseases: A Meta-Analysis. Nicotine & Tobacco Research. Across 57 studies: hypertension OR 1.28 (95% CI 1.15–1.40), heart disease 1.39 (95% CI 1.28–1.50), myocardial infarction 1.50 (95% CI 1.17–1.84), stroke 1.36 (95% CI 1.18–1.54); the association with cardiovascular disease was stronger for home exposure. <https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntaf111>
- Notes: Home is an important place to prevent exposure, both because of the time spent there and because the meta-analysis found a stronger association there. Children bore 61% of the estimated loss of healthy life measured in disability-adjusted life years. See recommendation 1 for quitting smoking yourself.

> Editorial clarification: Odds are not the same as probabilities. The home-versus-other-setting comparison does not prove that an equal dose of smoke is intrinsically more toxic at home; exposure duration and other differences may contribute. The mortality estimate refers to 2004.

### 3. Skip sugary drinks
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No additional spending; switching to water or unsweetened tea can save money.
- In plain language: In two US cohorts, drinking at least two servings of sugary drinks a day was associated with an all-cause mortality hazard about 21% higher than drinking less than one serving a month. At one to fewer than two servings daily, it was about 14% higher. These are associations over follow-up, not proof that the drinks alone caused the difference.
- Benefit: Two observational US cohorts, with approximately 118,000 participants and 36,000 deaths, found an all-cause mortality HR of 1.21 for at least 2 servings a day versus less than 1 a month, and 1.14 for 1 to fewer than 2 servings a day. In the observational European EPIC cohort of about 450,000 people, at least 2 glasses a day versus less than 1 a month was associated with an HR of 1.08 for sugar-sweetened soft drinks and 1.17 for soft drinks overall.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Malik VS et al. (2019). Long-Term Consumption of Sugar-Sweetened and Artificially Sweetened Beverages and Risk of Mortality in US Adults. Circulation. <https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.118.037401> ; Mullee A et al. (2019). Association Between Soft Drink Consumption and Mortality in 10 European Countries. JAMA Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.2478>
- Notes: These are observational data. People who drink a lot of sugary drinks can differ in other aspects of their lifestyle, which may inflate the estimated association. In EPIC, at least 2 glasses of artificially sweetened drinks a day was associated with an HR of 1.26, higher than for sugary drinks. Reverse causation is one possible explanation: people with metabolic health problems may switch to diet drinks. This is not proof that diet drinks are more harmful. Do not treat them as a reason to drink unlimited amounts; make water your usual choice.

> Editorial clarification: A study serving is not necessarily a bottle. EPIC used a glass of approximately 250 mL. The US category associated with HR 1.14 was 1 to fewer than 2 servings daily, rather than an overlapping “1–2.” Neither study randomly assigned drinks.

### 4. Don’t chew areca nut or betel quid
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No additional spending; you save what you would have spent on the product.
- In plain language: Studies linked chewing betel quid to about 21% higher all-cause mortality and roughly 50% higher risks of diabetes and metabolic syndrome. Those findings are separate from the established cancer concerns, including oral and esophageal cancer. Products without added tobacco are not harmless.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis of 17 Asian studies involving about 388,000 people found an all-cause mortality RR of 1.21 for betel-quid chewers compared with non-chewers (P = 0.02; about 179,600 participants in the mortality analysis), an RR of 1.47 for diabetes and 1.51 for metabolic syndrome. Areca nut/betel-quid chewing also carries established cancer risks, including oral cancer; the mixture, including whether tobacco is added, matters when interpreting the evidence for different cancer sites.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Yamada T, Hara K, Kadowaki T (2013). Chewing betel quid and the risk of metabolic disease, cardiovascular disease, and all-cause mortality: a meta-analysis. PLoS One, 8(8), e70679. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0070679>
- Notes: Oral-cancer risk increases with longer use and greater daily consumption; it decreases over time after stopping. Adding tobacco adds further harm. The metabolic and mortality estimates are associations, not guaranteed reductions that an individual will obtain after quitting.

> Editorial clarification: IARC distinguishes the evidence by product: betel quid without tobacco causes oral cancer; betel quid with tobacco also causes cancers of the pharynx and esophagus. Stopping areca products with or without tobacco reduces oral-cancer risk. [IARC assessment](https://publications.iarc.who.int/_publications/media/download/2691/8fbbe22749ff05e99bd470de1feffd227f02a890.pdf) and [cessation evidence](https://www.iarc.who.int/news-events/launch-of-iarc-handbooks-of-cancer-prevention-volume-19-oral-cancer-prevention/).

### 5. Consider replacing table salt with a lower-sodium potassium-containing salt, if it is suitable for you
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: About 3.20 USD for an 11 oz container in one US example or 7.75 EUR for 350 g in Germany. These are full package prices, not the extra cost over regular salt. Once you have changed the product, using it takes little extra effort; taste varies by formulation. [Products, prices and limitations](cost_examples.md).
- In plain language: In a randomized trial of about 20,000 high-risk adults, replacing ordinary household salt with a lower-sodium substitute reduced the death rate by about 12% and the stroke rate by about 14% over roughly five years. Random assignment makes this stronger evidence than an ordinary observational association. It does not mean that everyone should switch without checking whether extra potassium is safe for them.
- Benefit: In rural China, 20,995 people with a history of stroke or aged at least 60 with high blood pressure were followed for a mean of 4.74 years. Compared with regular salt, the substitute produced rate ratios of 0.88 for all-cause death, 0.86 for stroke and 0.87 for major cardiovascular events. Serious adverse events attributed to hyperkalemia did not differ significantly between groups.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Neal B et al. (2021). Effect of Salt Substitution on Cardiovascular Events and Death. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2105675>
- Notes: Disputed. In the observational PURE study (O’Donnell M et al., 2014. Urinary sodium and potassium excretion, mortality, and cardiovascular events. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1311889>), estimated sodium excretion below 3 g/day was associated with an OR of 1.27 for death or a cardiovascular event; at least 7 g/day with an OR of 1.15, compared with the reference range. This suggested a J-shaped association. SSaSS, however, was randomized and used partial sodium replacement, not an attempt to reduce sodium to the very low levels suggested by that observational comparison. Its participants were at high cardiovascular risk; younger, healthy people would generally have less absolute benefit. Ask a clinician before switching if you have impaired kidney function or take medicines that retain potassium.

> Editorial clarification: SSaSS used 75% sodium chloride and 25% potassium chloride; commercial products can have different proportions. The ratios concern event rates, not a fixed five-year probability. No significant difference in serious hyperkalemia does not establish safety for people excluded because of kidney disease or potassium-related risks. WHO’s 2025 recommendation is conditional and applies to adults, excluding pregnant women and people with kidney impairment or other conditions that compromise potassium excretion; it does not cover children. Do not increase the amount of salt you use. [WHO guideline](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240105591).

### 6. Brush carefully, clean between your teeth daily, and see a dentist about missing teeth
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: A 50 m floss pack is about 8.20 USD in one US listing or 2.45 EUR in Germany. Annual spending depends on the product and how much you use; allow another 2–3 minutes daily. Professional cleaning and tooth replacement cost extra and need a separate quote. [Price examples](cost_examples.md).
- In plain language: In a Japanese study of older adults, using floss or another interdental cleaner was associated with an all-cause mortality hazard about 11% lower. A separate review found higher odds of death among older adults with no teeth or fewer than 20 teeth. That does not show that flossing or replacing teeth will produce the same change in your chance of dying. Dental care matters for comfort, eating and oral health in its own right.
- Benefit: A 6-year Japanese cohort of 9,676 people found an all-cause mortality HR of 0.89 among people using interdental cleaning tools and 0.77 among those using tongue cleaners. In a meta-analysis of community-dwelling older adults, the odds ratio for death was 1.87 for having no teeth (95% CI 1.35–2.59) and 2.04 for having fewer than 20 teeth (1.67–2.49).
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Wang K, Matsuyama Y, Kiuchi S, et al. (2026). Routine oral health practices and all-cause mortality. Journal of Dentistry. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdent.2026.106789> ; Ko MJ, Seo S, So JS, et al. (2026). Deteriorated oral health and function as risk factors for physical disability and mortality in community-dwelling older adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis. European Geriatric Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s41999-025-01319-4>
- Notes: Disputed. These are observational data: poor oral health can accompany worse general health and financial circumstances, and the direction of causation is uncertain. Basic interdental care is inexpensive, while periodontal disease and missing teeth can themselves make eating difficult. The cost and appropriate way to replace a tooth require an individual dental assessment.

> Editorial clarification: The Japanese cohort consisted of independently living older adults with natural teeth; it excluded people with no teeth. HR 0.89 describes about an 11% lower hazard, while OR 1.87 and 2.04 describe odds, not 87% and 104% higher probabilities. These studies do not establish that tooth replacement reduces mortality.

### 7. Aim for 7,000–8,000 steps a day
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=多 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: About 60–90 minutes of walking, depending on your pace. You can fit it into your commute and shopping trips; walking itself costs nothing.
- In plain language: People averaging about 5,800 steps a day had an all-cause mortality hazard about 40% lower than those averaging 3,500; at about 7,800 steps, it was roughly 45% lower. The association leveled off at higher step counts, with the range depending on age. Among older adults, much of the observed association was already present at 6,000–8,000 steps daily.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis of 15 observational cohorts included 47,471 people and 3,013 deaths. Compared with a median of 3,553 steps daily in the lowest quartile, medians of 5,801, 7,842 and 10,901 corresponded to all-cause mortality HRs of 0.60, 0.55 and 0.47. The curve leveled off around 6,000–8,000 steps in people aged ≥60 and 8,000–10,000 in those aged <60. Another meta-analysis reported a lower mortality hazard above a reference level of about 3,867 steps daily, with approximately 15% lower hazard per additional 1,000 steps.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Paluch AE et al. (2022). Daily steps and all-cause mortality: a meta-analysis of 15 international cohorts. Lancet Public Health. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(21)00302-9> ; Banach M et al. (2023). The association between daily step count and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality: a meta-analysis. European Journal of Preventive Cardiology. <https://doi.org/10.1093/eurjpc/zwad229>
- Notes: These are observational findings. The least active groups include people who already have illnesses or frailty, so reverse causation can exaggerate the apparent benefit of walking. The dose-response pattern is clear: increasing from roughly 4,000 to 7,000 steps lies in a part of the curve with substantial differences in mortality. There is no need to fixate on 10,000. Recommendation 10 describes activity in minutes rather than steps; the same walk can contribute to both, without needing two separate exercise sessions.

> Editorial clarification: The 3,867-step figure is the median of a reference group, not a proven threshold below which walking has no benefit. Step counts and minutes of moderate activity are not interchangeable: the pace matters. The corrected cross-reference is to recommendation 10. Meeting a step target alone does not establish that you meet all activity recommendations, including muscle strengthening. [WHO guidance](https://www.who.int/europe/news-room/fact-sheets/item/physical-activity).

### 8. Take prescribed blood-pressure and cholesterol medicines regularly; don’t stop on your own
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Depends on the medicine, dose and coverage. A US pharmacy program advertises selected generic prescriptions from 4 USD for a 30-day supply; eligibility for your prescription must be checked. In Germany, people with statutory insurance generally pay 5–10 EUR per covered prescription pack, with exceptions. Neither is a price for a complete treatment plan. Build a daily routine; take each medicine as prescribed, including the frequency. [Prices and conditions](cost_examples.md).
- In plain language: In randomized trials, reducing systolic blood pressure by 10 mmHg reduced relative all-cause mortality risk by about 13% and stroke risk by about 27%. Statin treatment that lowered LDL cholesterol by 1 mmol/L reduced all-cause mortality by about 10%. Observational studies also found mortality roughly 30%–45% lower among people who took their medicines consistently than among those with poor adherence, but that comparison does not isolate the medicine’s effect.
- Benefit: Meta-analysis of randomized trials: per 10 mmHg reduction in systolic blood pressure, all-cause mortality RR 0.87, major cardiovascular events RR 0.80, stroke RR 0.73 and heart failure RR 0.72. In statin trials, per 1.0 mmol/L reduction in LDL cholesterol, the rate ratio was 0.90 for all-cause mortality and 0.78 for major vascular events. An observational adherence meta-analysis found all-cause mortality RRs of 0.55 for statins and 0.71 for blood-pressure medicines when comparing adherence ≥80% with poor adherence.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Ettehad D et al. (2016). Blood pressure lowering for prevention of cardiovascular disease and death: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(15)01225-8> ; Cholesterol Treatment Trialists’ (CTT) Collaboration (2010). Efficacy and safety of more intensive lowering of LDL cholesterol: a meta-analysis of data from 170 000 participants in 26 randomised trials. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(10)61350-5> ; Chowdhury R et al. (2013). Adherence to cardiovascular therapy: a meta-analysis of prevalence and clinical consequences. European Heart Journal. <https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/eht295>
- Notes: This advice applies when there is a medical indication for treatment; these are not medicines to start simply because you feel healthy and want to live longer. People who consistently take prescribed medicines may also have other healthier habits, inflating the observational adherence estimates. The first two sets of randomized-trial results provide stronger evidence of treatment effects. Intensive glucose lowering is not listed separately here because its all-cause mortality evidence is less consistent than that for blood-pressure and cholesterol treatment.

> Editorial clarification: A medical indication can include prevention before a first cardiovascular event; feeling well does not rule it out. Relative effects are not percentage-point reductions and cannot be added together to predict your benefit. The statement about intensive glucose lowering is not advice to stop diabetes treatment. Discuss side effects or a change in treatment with your prescriber.

### 9. Keep a regular sleep schedule and allow enough time to sleep
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=多 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Time—for many people, this means replacing late-night phone use with sleep. Keeping a consistent schedule takes some effort.
- In plain language: Studies associated habitual short sleep, often defined as less than 7 hours, with roughly 10% higher mortality. More regular sleep was also associated with lower mortality: the four more regular groups had hazards 20%–48% lower than the least regular group. In that cohort, regularity predicted mortality better than duration did; it does not follow that getting enough sleep matters less.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis of 16 observational studies, about 1.38 million people and 113,000 deaths, found all-cause mortality RRs of 1.12 for short sleep and 1.30 for long sleep. A dose-response meta-analysis found the lowest observed risk around 7 hours: below 7 hours, RR 1.06 per 1-hour reduction; above 7 hours, RR 1.13 per 1-hour increase. In an observational UK Biobank study of about 61,000 people with wrist-device data, the four more regular sleep quintiles had all-cause mortality hazards 20%–48% lower than the least regular quintile. Regularity was a stronger statistical predictor than duration.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Cappuccio FP et al. (2010). Sleep duration and all-cause mortality: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective studies. Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/33.5.585> ; Yin J et al. (2017). Relationship of Sleep Duration With All-Cause Mortality and Cardiovascular Events: A Systematic Review and Dose-Response Meta-Analysis of Prospective Cohort Studies. JAHA. <https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.117.005947> ; Windred DP et al. (2024). Sleep regularity is a stronger predictor of mortality risk than sleep duration: A prospective cohort study. Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsad253>
- Notes: Reverse causation is a plausible explanation for part of the association with long sleep: depression, chronic illness and sleep apnea can affect sleep and health. Do not deliberately shorten needed sleep to improve a statistic. Short sleep and irregular schedules are more useful targets for change. The regularity estimate cited here comes from a single cohort and, considered on its own, has evidence grade B.

> Editorial clarification: Around seven hours was the lowest point of an observational curve, not a prescription for everyone. Sleep needs vary; US NIH guidance recommends 7–9 hours for adults. The 20%–48% range covers four groups, not just the single most regular group. [NIH sleep guidance](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep/how-much-sleep).

### 10. Get 150–300 minutes of moderate activity each week; brisk walking counts
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=多 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Roughly 20–45 minutes a day; brisk walking need not cost anything. Check your weekly total: 20 minutes every day adds up to 140, just below 150.
- In plain language: Activity equivalent to 150–300 minutes of brisk walking a week was associated with an all-cause mortality hazard about 30% lower than no leisure-time activity. Even less activity was associated with about 20% lower hazard. At three to five times the minimum recommended amount, the difference approached 40%; beyond that, the study found no further clear benefit and no excess mortality compared with inactivity.
- Benefit: A pooled observational analysis found an all-cause mortality HR of 0.69 for 1–2 times the guideline minimum—7.5–15 MET-hours/week, roughly 150–300 minutes of brisk walking—versus no leisure-time activity. Below the minimum, HR was 0.80. At 3–5 times the minimum, HR reached 0.61; greater amounts did not show additional benefit, but even ≥10 times the minimum was not associated with excess mortality versus inactivity (HR 0.69). An accelerometer-based meta-analysis found HR 0.52 for the highest versus lowest quartile of moderate-to-vigorous activity.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Arem H et al. (2015). Leisure time physical activity and mortality: a detailed pooled analysis of the dose-response relationship. JAMA Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2015.0533> ; Ekelund U et al. (2019). Dose-response associations between accelerometry measured physical activity and sedentary time and all cause mortality: systematic review and harmonised meta-analysis. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.l4570>
- Notes: These are observational data. The accelerometer studies generally had relatively short follow-up and older participants; reverse causation may explain part of the large association represented by HR 0.52. The true causal benefit may be smaller, which would put the HR closer to 1. Recommendation 7 offers another way to track walking. You can count a brisk walk toward both goals rather than adding a separate session for each.

> Editorial clarification: “No excess mortality detected” does not mean unlimited exercise is free of injury or other harm. MET-hours combine activity intensity and duration; step counts alone do not determine intensity. The corrected cross-reference is to recommendation 7. WHO also recommends muscle-strengthening activity on at least two days a week; neither step count nor aerobic minutes replace that component. [WHO guidance](https://www.who.int/europe/news-room/fact-sheets/item/physical-activity).

### 11. Play a racket sport three times a week for 45 minutes
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Court rental varies. One US badminton venue charges 35–50 USD per court-hour; a Berlin example is 19 EUR per court-hour on weekday mornings and early afternoons. Split the court fee with your playing partners; equipment may cost extra. Three 45-minute sessions take 2 hours 15 minutes a week, plus travel and preparation. [Prices and conditions](cost_examples.md).
- In plain language: In a British cohort, people who played tennis, badminton or squash had an all-cause mortality hazard nearly half that of people who did not play those sports; their cardiovascular mortality hazard was about 56% lower. Swimming was associated with about 28% lower all-cause mortality. Running and soccer did not show statistically significant associations in this dataset; that does not mean they provide no benefit.
- Benefit: In a British cohort of about 80,300 adults, racket sports—tennis, badminton and squash—were associated with an all-cause mortality HR of 0.53 (95% CI 0.40–0.69) and a cardiovascular mortality HR of 0.44 (0.24–0.83), compared with not playing those sports. For swimming, the corresponding HRs were 0.72 and 0.59; for aerobics, 0.73 and 0.64. Cycling had an all-cause mortality HR of 0.85. Running and soccer did not show statistically significant associations.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Oja P, Kelly P, Pedisic Z, et al. (2017). Associations of specific types of sports and exercise with all-cause and cardiovascular-disease mortality: a cohort study of 80 306 British adults. British Journal of Sports Medicine, 51(10), 812-817. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2016-096822>
- Notes: Disputed. This was an observational study: people who play sports may already be healthier and have more social contact. Selection effects may influence comparisons between sports. The nonsignificant running result is not a reason to dismiss running; the overall amount of activity is covered elsewhere in this chapter.

> Editorial correction: The study grouped badminton, tennis and squash together; it did not establish these estimates for table tennis. The schedule in the heading is a practical suggestion, not a regimen shown to halve mortality. Few deaths among runners and soccer players limited the precision of those estimates. [Study text](https://www.usta.com/content/dam/usta/sections/northern/pdf/hainline-interview/tennis-benefits-bjsm.pdf).

### 12. Add up four or five minutes of brief, vigorous activity during your day
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No money or separate workout time. Examples include climbing stairs or walking fast when getting somewhere.
- In plain language: Among people who reported no regular exercise, three short bursts of vigorous everyday activity a day were associated with roughly 40% lower all-cause mortality and nearly 50% lower cardiovascular mortality, compared with no such activity. The bursts were brief—up to one or two minutes in the analyses. This was an association during follow-up, not a guaranteed effect of starting that routine.
- Benefit: A UK Biobank analysis of about 25,200 nonexercisers recorded 852 deaths over an average 6.9 years. 3 daily bouts of vigorous intermittent lifestyle physical activity, analyzed using bouts lasting up to 1–2 minutes, were associated with 38%–40% lower all-cause and cancer mortality and 48%–49% lower cardiovascular mortality than no such activity. A daily total of 4.4 minutes was associated with 26%–30% lower all-cause and cancer mortality and 32%–34% lower cardiovascular mortality.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Stamatakis E, Ahmadi MN, Gill JMR, et al. (2022). Association of wearable device-measured vigorous intermittent lifestyle physical activity with mortality. Nature Medicine, 28, 2521-2529. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-022-02100-x>
- Notes: Disputed. Wearable devices measured activity more objectively than questionnaires, but this was still observational research with only 6.9 years of follow-up. The main analysis concerned people who did not exercise regularly; its estimates should not automatically be applied to people who already do.

> Editorial clarification: The paper also analyzed exercisers and found similar associations with brief vigorous activity. “Nonexerciser” does not mean someone never moves. Bout frequency and total minutes were analyzed separately; the two percentage ranges are not interchangeable or additive. Choose an intensity you can manage rather than turning every staircase into an all-out effort.

### 13. Do 30–60 minutes of strength training each week
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: One or two sessions of 20–30 minutes; bodyweight squats and push-ups need no paid equipment. Check the total: one 20-minute session does not reach the range in the heading.
- In plain language: People who did strength training had an all-cause mortality risk about 15% lower than those who did none. The largest differences appeared around 30–60 minutes a week; greater amounts did not show a clear additional mortality benefit. People who combined strength training with aerobic activity also had lower mortality.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis of observational cohorts associated strength training with 10%–17% lower risks across several outcomes, including all-cause mortality and major chronic diseases. The all-cause mortality estimate was RR 0.85, or about 15% lower risk. Dose-response curves for mortality and some other outcomes were J-shaped, with the largest reductions, roughly 10%–20%, around 30–60 minutes/week. More training did not show a clear further benefit in those curves. Combining strength and aerobic activity was associated with lower all-cause mortality than doing neither.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Momma H et al. (2022). Muscle-strengthening activities are associated with lower risk and mortality in major non-communicable diseases: a systematic review and meta-analysis of cohort studies. British Journal of Sports Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2021-105061>
- Notes: The studies were observational and relied on self-reported activity. Evidence for the rising right-hand side of the J-shaped curve was sparse; do not use it to impose an upper limit on training. Older adults also benefit from maintaining muscle and preventing falls, as discussed in chapter 1.

> Editorial clarification: The 10%–17% range covers several different outcomes; the estimate for all-cause mortality alone was about 15%. The review rated the mortality evidence as very low certainty under GRADE. The book’s evidence grade A uses a different classification and does not mean that this causal effect is certain. [Review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9209691/).

### 14. Break up long periods of sitting with some movement
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Nothing; set a reminder if it helps.
- In plain language: In a US cohort, people with the most sedentary time had an all-cause mortality hazard about 2.6 times that of those with the least. Longer uninterrupted sedentary periods were also associated with higher mortality. In another analysis, people doing about 60–75 minutes of moderate activity daily showed little difference in mortality across sitting-time groups. That does not prove that exercise cancels every consequence of sitting.
- Benefit: In an observational US accelerometer study, the highest versus lowest quartile of total sedentary time had an all-cause mortality HR of 2.63. For average uninterrupted bout length, the highest versus lowest quartile had HR 1.96. Both associations remained after adjustment for factors including moderate-to-vigorous activity. A pooled observational analysis of over a million people found HR 1.59 for sitting >8 hours/day with very little activity, compared with sitting <4 hours/day in the most active group. In the most active group, equivalent to about 60–75 minutes of moderate activity daily, sitting >8 hours/day had HR 1.04 versus the reference group, a nonsignificant difference.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Diaz KM et al. (2017). Patterns of Sedentary Behavior and Mortality in U.S. Middle-Aged and Older Adults: A National Cohort Study. Annals of Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.7326/M17-0212> ; Ekelund U et al. (2016). Does physical activity attenuate, or even eliminate, the detrimental association of sitting time with mortality? A harmonised meta-analysis of data from more than 1 million men and women. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(16)30370-1>
- Notes: These are observational findings. Illness and frailty can lead people to sit more, so reverse causation may contribute to an estimate as large as HR 2.63. Overall activity matters greatly: sufficiently active groups did not show a statistically significant excess mortality associated with longer sitting. Television viewing differed: ≥3 hours/day was associated with higher mortality at most activity levels; in the most active group, a significant association appeared at ≥5 hours/day (HR 1.16).

> Editorial clarification: The HRs for total sedentary time and bout length describe separate comparisons, not two effects to add or multiply. A nonsignificant difference does not establish zero harm. These studies did not test whether reminders or a particular schedule of breaks reduces mortality. The amount attributable to reverse causation cannot be read from HR 2.63 alone.

### 15. Eat less processed meat: ham, bacon, sausages and luncheon meat
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No extra spending; you may save money. The trade-off is giving up some familiar flavors.
- In plain language: In observational studies, people who ate the most processed meat had a risk of dying during follow-up about one-fifth higher than those who ate the least. A separate analysis linked each additional daily serving to a similar increase in risk.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis of observational studies comparing the highest and lowest intake groups found all-cause mortality RRs of 1.23 for processed meat, 1.29 for total red meat and 1.10 for unprocessed red meat; the last estimate was not statistically significant. Another meta-analysis examined intake by amount: each additional serving per day was associated with an RR of 1.23 for processed meat and 1.10 for red meat.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Larsson SC, Orsini N (2014). Red meat and processed meat consumption and all-cause mortality: a meta-analysis. American Journal of Epidemiology. <https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwt261> ; Schwingshackl L et al. (2017). Food groups and risk of all-cause mortality: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective studies. American Journal of Clinical Nutrition. <https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.117.153148>
- Notes: The evidence is disputed. The NutriRECS guideline (Johnston BC et al., 2019. Unprocessed Red Meat and Processed Meat Consumption: Dietary Guideline Recommendations From the NutriRECS Consortium. Annals of Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.7326/M19-1621>) rated its certainty as low under GRADE and made a weak recommendation to maintain current intake. The disagreement concerns how convincing the evidence is and whether it warrants changing habits; the studies cited here do not show that processed meat is beneficial. The association for unprocessed red meat is smaller and was not statistically significant in the first meta-analysis. Processed meat is the main focus here.

> Editorial clarification: The second meta-analysis defined a serving as 50 g of processed meat or 100 g of red meat. Its red-meat estimate was statistically significant; the first analysis’s nonsignificant result should not be applied to both. Observational associations alone do not establish a causal effect. [Methods and results of the second meta-analysis](https://r.jordan.im/download/nutrition/schwingshackl2017.pdf).

### 16. Drink less alcohol, or none
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Turning down a drink can feel awkward socially; it also saves money.
- In plain language: Among people who drank alcohol, consuming more than 100 g of pure alcohol a week—roughly 2.5 L of 5% beer—was associated with a shorter life. For a person aged 40, more than 100 and up to 200 g a week corresponded to about half a year less life expectancy, more than 200 and up to 350 g to one or two years less, and more than 350 g to four or five years less, compared with drinking up to 100 g. The GBD 2016 analysis, which combined different kinds of health loss, estimated the lowest overall harm at no alcohol consumption.
- Benefit: A pooled observational analysis of 83 prospective studies, with about 600,000 current drinkers, found the lowest all-cause mortality at ≤100 g of pure alcohol a week—roughly 2.5 L of 5% beer or 300 mL of 40% spirits. Compared with drinking >0–≤100 g a week, life expectancy at age 40 was about 6 months shorter at >100–200 g, 1–2 years shorter at >200–350 g and 4–5 years shorter at >350 g; the outcome was reported in years of life. GBD 2016 estimated the lowest combined risk across health outcomes at 0 drinks a week. A meta-analysis that adjusted for study biases found an all-cause mortality RR of 0.93 for 1.3–24 g a day versus lifetime nondrinkers, a nonsignificant difference; the RR was 1.19 at 45–64 g and 1.35 at ≥65 g a day.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Wood AM et al. (2018). Risk thresholds for alcohol consumption: combined analysis of individual-participant data for 599 912 current drinkers in 83 prospective studies. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)30134-X> ; GBD 2016 Alcohol Collaborators (2018). Alcohol use and burden for 195 countries and territories, 1990–2016: a systematic analysis for the Global Burden of Disease Study 2016. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31310-2> ; Zhao J et al. (2023). Association Between Daily Alcohol Intake and Risk of All-Cause Mortality: A Systematic Review and Meta-analyses. JAMA Network Open. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2023.6185>
- Notes: This remains disputed. Support for a J-shaped curve includes a meta-analysis of 34 prospective studies (Di Castelnuovo A et al., 2006. Alcohol dosing and total mortality in men and women: an updated meta-analysis of 34 prospective studies. Archives of Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.166.22.2437>): low intake was associated with up to 17%–18% lower all-cause mortality, with an inverse association persisting at ≤4 drinks a day for men and ≤2 for women. Critics point out that nondrinker groups could include people who stopped because of illness and people with poorer health to begin with. After adjustments for these biases, Zhao 2023 found no significant protective association; the GBD analysis published in 2018 also put the minimum at 0. The practical conclusion is that high intake is clearly associated with harm. Low intake may show no significant difference in all-cause mortality, but that does not establish safety. There is no basis for starting to drink for your health.

> Editorial clarification: The 100 g threshold compares people who already drink; it is neither a safe allowance nor evidence of an advantage over abstinence. The drink counts from the 2006 paper are historical findings, not current recommended limits. If you are dependent on alcohol, stopping abruptly can be dangerous. Get medical help, especially if cutting down causes shaking, sweating or other withdrawal symptoms. [NHS guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/alcohol-use-disorder/).

### 17. Eat a small handful of nuts each day
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: At 28 g a day, you would eat about 10.22 kg a year. Two walnut price examples work out to about 224 USD or 112 EUR a year, excluding delivery and any additional tax. These are calculations for specific products, not minimum budgets; prices vary by nut, brand and store. [Prices, availability and calculation](cost_examples.md).
- In plain language: People who regularly ate nuts had an all-cause mortality hazard during follow-up about one-fifth lower than people who ate none. A difference was apparent at one serving a week—about one-tenth lower—and was largest among those who ate nuts daily or more often.
- Benefit: Two US cohorts included about 119,000 people and more than 3 million person-years of follow-up. Compared with eating no nuts, the all-cause mortality HR was 0.93 (95% CI 0.90–0.96) for less than 1 serving a week; 0.89 (0.86–0.93) for 1; 0.87 (0.83–0.90) for 2–4; 0.85 (0.79–0.91) for 5–6; and 0.80 (0.73–0.86) for 7 or more servings a week.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Bao Y, Han J, Hu FB, et al. (2013). Association of nut consumption with total and cause-specific mortality. New England Journal of Medicine, 369(21), 2001-2011. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1307352>
- Notes: These were observational cohorts. People who ate nuts generally had healthier lifestyles, so nuts themselves may explain only part of the difference. Choose plain, unsalted nuts. They are calorie-dense; do not treat them as an unlimited snack.

### 18. Replace some red meat with fish and poultry
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: There need not be any extra spending: replace part of your purchase rather than adding more food. Choose fish or poultry within your existing budget; the substitution will not always cost exactly the same.
- In plain language: Each additional two servings of processed or red meat a week was associated with an all-cause mortality hazard about 3% higher. The same increase in fish or poultry intake had no statistically significant association with mortality. This is a small difference: make the switch when convenient without worrying about every serving.
- Benefit: A pooled analysis of 6 US cohorts, with nearly 30,000 participants, found an all-cause mortality HR of 1.03 (95% CI 1.02–1.05) for each additional 2 servings of processed meat a week, and 1.03 (1.01–1.05) for unprocessed red meat. For the same increase in poultry intake, the HR was 0.99 (0.97–1.02); for fish, 0.99 (0.97–1.01). Neither of the latter associations was statistically significant.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Zhong VW, Van Horn L, Greenland P, et al. (2020). Associations of Processed Meat, Unprocessed Red Meat, Poultry, or Fish Intake With Incident Cardiovascular Disease and All-Cause Mortality. JAMA Internal Medicine, 180(4), 503-512. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.6969>
- Notes: The difference for two servings a week is very small; do not expect a meat substitution alone to extend your life. This recommendation helps you choose what to buy when you are already spending money on meat.

> Editorial clarification: These figures describe separate associations with each food’s intake. They do not come from a trial in which people replaced red meat with fish or poultry. A nonsignificant association does not establish zero risk, and a relative 3% is not a three-percentage-point change in your personal probability of dying.

### 19. Replace some white rice and refined-flour foods with whole grains
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Brown rice, oats and whole-wheat flour may cost a little more than your usual choices. Their taste and texture can take some getting used to.
- In plain language: In observational studies, each additional 90 g of whole-grain foods a day—about three servings—was associated with a risk of dying during follow-up about 17% lower. The association with lower risk continued at intakes around 200 g a day.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis of observational studies found an all-cause mortality RR of 0.83 per additional 90 g a day, or about 3 servings. The dose-response curve continued downward up to 210–225 g a day. Another meta-analysis found an RR of 0.92 per additional 1 serving a day.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Aune D et al. (2016). Whole grain consumption and risk of cardiovascular disease, cancer, and all cause and cause specific mortality: systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of prospective studies. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.i2716> ; Schwingshackl L et al. (2017). Food groups and risk of all-cause mortality: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective studies. American Journal of Clinical Nutrition. <https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.117.153148>
- Notes: These are observational data. People who eat whole grains may have healthier lifestyles overall, so the causal effect of the food itself may be smaller than the observed difference. Results varied substantially between studies: I² = 83%. You do not need to change everything at once; try replacing half your usual grain foods. The steepest part of the curve was at increases from low initial intake.

> Editorial clarification: Replacing half is a practical starting point, not a dose tested in a trial; its effect depends on what and how much you currently eat. The first meta-analysis standardized grain-food servings to 30 g. Do not automatically treat these amounts as the weight of pure whole-grain ingredients or cooked porridge.

### 20. Drink tea at least three times a week
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: At one tea bag a day, two retail examples work out to about 25 USD or 45 EUR a year for the tea itself. These are calculations for specific products, not minimum budgets. [Prices and conditions](cost_examples.md).
- In plain language: People who regularly drank tea had an all-cause mortality hazard during follow-up about 15% lower. Estimates starting at age 50 put their life expectancy 1.26 years higher and their time without atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease 1.41 years longer.
- Benefit: The Chinese China-PAR cohort included about 100,900 people, with a median follow-up of 7.3 years. Habitual tea drinkers had an all-cause mortality HR of 0.85 (95% CI 0.79–0.90). Starting at age 50, estimated years free of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease were 1.41 years greater, and life expectancy was 1.26 years greater.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Wang X, Liu F, Li J, et al. (2020). Tea consumption and the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality: The China-PAR project. European Journal of Preventive Cardiology, 27(18), 1956-1963. <https://doi.org/10.1177/2047487319894685>
- Notes: Disputed. This was observational research. Smoking and alcohol use were common among Chinese men who drank tea; statistical adjustments addressed those differences, but residual confounding remains possible. Do not drink scalding tea; a separate recommendation in this chapter covers drink temperature.

> Editorial clarification: Habitual drinking meant at least three times a week; the comparison group drank tea less often or never. The additional years are modeled estimates from cohort data, not a proven gain from taking up tea drinking.

### 21. Drink coffee without sugar or sweet cream toppings; three or four cups a day will not suit everyone
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: The home instant-coffee examples cost about 0.16–0.22 USD or 0.27–0.36 EUR a day for 6–8 g of coffee. This assumes 2 g per cup and three or four cups, solely for budgeting; it is not the study dose. Water and energy costs are excluded. [Prices and calculation](cost_examples.md).
- In plain language: People who drank three or four cups of coffee a day had a risk of dying during follow-up about 17% lower than people who drank none. This observational association is not a reason to make yourself drink coffee for a longer life.
- Benefit: An umbrella review that included 201 meta-analyses of observational studies found an all-cause mortality RR of 0.83 (95% CI 0.79–0.88) for 3–4 cups a day versus none, or a 17% lower risk.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Poole R, Kennedy OJ, Roderick P, et al. (2017). Coffee consumption and health: umbrella review of meta-analyses of multiple health outcomes. BMJ, 359, j5024. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.j5024>
- Notes: Disputed: almost all the evidence was observational. Reliable randomized trials are needed to determine causation. Pregnancy, arrhythmias, anxiety or insomnia can change what is appropriate; discuss caffeine intake with your clinician. Sugar and sweet cream toppings add calories and can work against your dietary goals.

> Editorial clarification: The review does not establish that sugar or cream completely cancels coffee’s association with lower mortality. The confidence interval above incorporates the journal’s [2018 correction](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.k194). Cup size and strength varied between studies. The [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much) says up to 400 mg of caffeine a day from all sources is generally not associated with negative effects for most adults, but sensitivity varies. That is not a target or a blanket allowance for four large mugs.

### 22. Eat at least 5 servings—about 400 g—of fruit and vegetables each day
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Examples of buying 400 g of fruit and vegetables come to about 0.98 USD or 1.02 EUR before allowing for peeling and other waste. These price specific combinations, not a varied daily menu. Allow time for washing and chopping. [Foods, prices and limitations](cost_examples.md).
- In plain language: Each additional 200 g of fruit and vegetables a day was associated with a risk of dying during follow-up about one-tenth lower. In another study, people who ate five daily servings rather than two had a hazard about 13% lower; that study found no further reduction beyond five.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis of observational studies found an all-cause mortality RR of 0.90 per additional 200 g a day, with the dose-response curve continuing downward up to 800 g a day. An analysis of two large US cohorts found an all-cause mortality HR of 0.87 for 5 versus 2 daily servings. The same paper also included a meta-analysis of 26 cohorts. Mortality was lowest at about 2 servings of fruit and 3 of vegetables; higher intake was not associated with a further reduction.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Aune D et al. (2017). Fruit and vegetable intake and the risk of cardiovascular disease, total cancer and all-cause mortality: a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of prospective studies. International Journal of Epidemiology. <https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyw319> ; Wang DD et al. (2021). Fruit and Vegetable Intake and Mortality: Results From 2 Prospective Cohort Studies of US Men and Women and a Meta-Analysis of 26 Cohort Studies. Circulation. <https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.120.048996>
- Notes: These are observational data with substantial potential for confounding: people with different vegetable intakes also differ in income, education and physical activity. RR 0.90 may overstate the causal effect. Start with five servings; there is no need to reach ten at any cost. The studies do differ on additional benefit at higher intakes: one found an association up to 800 g, while the other found a plateau around five servings.

> Editorial clarification: RR 0.90 is an association estimate, not an established upper bound on the possible effect. Wang’s 26-cohort meta-analysis includes the two US cohorts; they are not additional to the 26. The 400 g target is consistent with [WHO guidance](https://www.who.int/health-topics/healthy-diet) for adults. Here, five servings means five 80 g portions of edible food; individual studies could use different serving sizes. These papers do not establish five servings as a universal ceiling on benefit.

### 23. Eat fewer ultra-processed foods, such as chips, instant noodles, packaged pastries and ready meals
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=多 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: You may need to cook more often or choose basic ingredients. This takes time and some persistence.
- In plain language: People who ate the most ultra-processed food had a risk of dying during follow-up about one-fifth higher than those who ate the least. For cardiovascular death, the difference was about one-half. Confidence in these estimates is low, though: do not treat them as a precise measure of harm caused by the foods themselves.
- Benefit: An umbrella review of observational meta-analyses found an all-cause mortality RR of 1.21 and a cardiovascular mortality RR of 1.50 for high versus low ultra-processed food intake. The review classified the statistical evidence for these associations as “highly suggestive” and “convincing,” respectively. GRADE certainty was nevertheless low and very low, respectively.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Lane MM et al. (2024). Ultra-processed food exposure and adverse health outcomes: umbrella review of epidemiological meta-analyses. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-077310>
- Notes: Disputed. The NOVA classification groups foods with very different nutritional profiles together. There is substantial overlap with sugary drinks and processed meat in recommendations 3 and 15, making an independent effect hard to isolate; GRADE certainty is low. If you already follow recommendations 3 and 15, the additional benefit of this advice is uncertain. The review’s own low-certainty ratings are one reason for caution. They do not establish the opposite conclusion—that ultra-processed foods are beneficial.

> Editorial clarification: Cross-references have been corrected to recommendations 3 and 15. A is a grade on this book’s scale; it does not mean high GRADE certainty. A statistically convincing association does not establish causation, either. Low certainty gives no numerical rule for reducing an effect estimate. This review cannot establish that the entire research literature contains no studies with different findings.

### 24. Avoid sweetened soft drinks, including sugar-free versions
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No extra spending; you may save money on drinks.
- In plain language: People who drank at least two glasses of soft drinks a day had an all-cause mortality hazard during follow-up about 17% higher than people who drank less than one glass a month. For artificially sweetened drinks, the difference was about 26%—larger than for sugary drinks. This does not show that sweeteners are more harmful: weight and health may influence drink choices. It also does not establish sugar-free drinks as a risk-free substitute.
- Benefit: A European cohort of about 450,000 people in 10 countries found an all-cause mortality HR of 1.17 (95% CI 1.11–1.22) for ≥2 glasses of soft drinks a day versus <1 glass a month. The HR was 1.08 (1.01–1.16) for sugar-sweetened drinks and 1.26 (1.16–1.35) for artificially sweetened drinks.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Mullee A, Romaguera D, Pearson-Stuttard J, et al. (2019). Association Between Soft Drink Consumption and Mortality in 10 European Countries. JAMA Internal Medicine, 179(11), 1479-1490. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.2478>
- Notes: Disputed. Reverse causation may partly explain the stronger association for artificially sweetened drinks: excess weight or worsening health could prompt a switch to sugar-free drinks. The study did not establish this as the main explanation, however. People with known diabetes at enrollment were excluded, so switching by people with diagnosed diabetes cannot simply account for the result. These data neither support the promise that replacing sugar with a sweetener removes all risk nor prove that making the switch is harmful.

> Editorial clarification: One glass meant approximately 250 mL. The high-intake category included exactly two glasses a day, not only more than two. This was an observational study, not a sugar-replacement trial; comparing the separate HRs for different drink types does not establish which drink is safer for an individual.

### 25. Switch from coal or wood to electricity or gas for cooking and heating
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=多 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Equipment upfront, then energy bills. Two examples of single-zone portable electric cooktops cost about 75 USD or 39 EUR. These are one-time appliance prices, not annual budgets or the cost of replacing a heating system. Allow separately for energy, delivery, installation work and any cookware you need. If you already cook and heat your home without coal or wood, no fuel switch is needed. [Prices, limitations and running-cost calculation](cost_examples.md).
- In plain language: People who cooked with coal or wood had an all-cause mortality hazard during follow-up about one-tenth higher; those who used these fuels for heating had a hazard about 14% higher. People who had switched to electricity or gas before follow-up had a hazard 13–33% lower than those who kept using solid fuels. The study cannot attribute the entire difference to the fuel switch itself.
- Benefit: An observational Chinese cohort included about 271,000 adults without cardiovascular disease. Solid versus cleaner fuel use was associated with an all-cause mortality HR of 1.11 for cooking and 1.14 for heating. Among people who had switched from solid to cleaner fuels, compared with continuing solid-fuel users, the HRs were 0.87 for cooking and 0.67 for heating. For outdoor air, a review of 107 studies, including 104 cohorts, found a natural-cause mortality RR of 1.08 per additional 10 µg/m³ of long-term PM2.5 exposure.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Yu K et al. (2018). Association of Solid Fuel Use With Risk of Cardiovascular and All-Cause Mortality in Rural China. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2018.2151> ; Chen J, Hoek G (2020). Long-term exposure to PM and all-cause and cause-specific mortality: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Environment International. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envint.2020.105974>
- Notes: These are observational data. Households that changed fuels may differ in income and other living conditions; the HR of 0.67 may still reflect confounding. Individuals have limited control over outdoor PM2.5. Moving, wearing a suitable respirator or using an air purifier may reduce exposure in different circumstances, but the cited studies provide no mortality-reduction estimate for a home air purifier. None is given here.

> Editorial clarification: “Cleaner fuel” means cleaner than coal or wood, not pollution-free. [WHO](https://www.who.int/tools/clean-household-energy-solutions-toolkit/module-7-defining-clean) classifies gas as clean for particle and carbon monoxide emissions at the point of use; gas equipment still needs proper installation and ventilation. The 104 figure counts cohort studies within the review, not a single pooled sample. Outdoor PM2.5 findings cannot quantify the effect of buying a cooktop or air purifier.

### 26. Let tea, soup and coffee cool; do not drink them scalding hot
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No money. Wait another two or three minutes, and longer if the drink is still scalding.
- In plain language: In a study in northern Iran, very hot tea was associated with roughly eight times the odds of esophageal cancer compared with warm tea; hot tea with about twice the odds. Drinking tea less than two minutes after pouring was associated with about five times the odds compared with waiting at least four minutes. Those are not equivalent increases in the probability of getting cancer, nor do they show that waiting two minutes is enough for everyone.
- Benefit: A case-control study in a high-incidence area of northern Iran included 300 cases and 571 controls. Compared with warm tea, the odds ratio for esophageal squamous cell carcinoma was 2.07 (95% CI 1.28–3.35) for hot tea and 8.16 (3.93–16.9) for very hot tea. Drinking less than 2 minutes after pouring versus waiting at least 4 minutes had an OR of 5.41 (2.63–11.1).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Islami F, Pourshams A, Nasrollahzadeh D, et al. (2009). Tea drinking habits and oesophageal cancer in a high risk area in northern Iran: population based case-control study. BMJ, 338, b929. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.b929> ; Loomis D, Guyton KZ, Grosse Y, et al. (2016). Carcinogenicity of drinking coffee, mate, and very hot beverages. Lancet Oncology, 17(7), 877-878. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S1470-2045(16)30239-X>
- Notes: The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies drinking beverages above 65 °C as Group 2A—probably carcinogenic to humans. Coffee drinking is in Group 3: the available evidence does not allow its carcinogenicity to be classified. That is not proof that coffee cannot cause cancer. High esophageal cancer rates in China’s Chaoshan and Taihang regions have been discussed in connection with drinking scalding beverages; that association alone does not explain all regional differences.

> Editorial clarification: This study measured cancer odds, not mortality. An odds ratio is not a risk ratio. It does not establish that waiting two minutes removes most of the risk: cooling depends on temperature, volume and the container. [IARC’s explanation of hot beverages and coffee](https://www.iarc.who.int/en/media-centre/pr/2016/pdfs/pr244_E.pdf) distinguishes probable carcinogenicity of very hot beverages from insufficient evidence to classify coffee drinking.

### 27. Get outdoors in daylight; do not spend the whole day away from natural light
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No money. Start with a short walk of ten to twenty minutes, perhaps during your commute or lunch break. This is a practical way to find time, not a sun-exposure dose established by the study.
- In plain language: In a Swedish study, women who avoided the sun had an estimated life expectancy 0.6–2.1 years shorter than women with the most sun exposure. Nonsmokers who avoided the sun had an estimate similar to smokers in the highest-exposure group. This compares groups; it does not show that a walk offsets smoking.
- Benefit: A Swedish cohort of about 29,500 women followed for 20 years found an estimated life expectancy 0.6–2.1 years shorter among sun avoiders than among those with the most sun exposure. Nonsmokers who avoided the sun had an estimate comparable to smokers in the highest-exposure group.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Lindqvist PG, Epstein E, Nielsen K, et al. (2016). Avoidance of sun exposure as a risk factor for major causes of death: a competing risk analysis of the Melanoma in Southern Sweden cohort. Journal of Internal Medicine, 280(4), 375-387. <https://doi.org/10.1111/joim.12496>
- Notes: Disputed. Sweden is at a high latitude with relatively little sunlight; the result cannot automatically be applied to China, the United States, the rest of Europe or any other region. People may have avoided the sun because they were already frail or rarely left home. UV exposure increases skin cancer risk. Avoid sunburn and protect yourself from strong midday sun.

> Editorial clarification: This study does not isolate the benefits of daylight or outdoor activity from all lifestyle differences, nor establish a safe tanning duration. The smoking comparison does not demonstrate equal causal harm. [WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/ultraviolet-radiation) recommends sun protection at a UV index of 3 or above: shade, covering clothing and sunscreen on exposed skin. There is no need to tan in pursuit of longevity.

### 28. Keep track of your weight: use BMI 20–25 as a reference and address excess weight
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=多 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: One of the most demanding recommendations in terms of sustained effort: changes to diet and physical activity need to last. Maintaining weight loss is often harder still. A paid program is not required, though medically indicated treatment can involve costs.
- In plain language: In a large analysis, mortality was lowest at a body mass index, or BMI, of 20–25. At a BMI of 27.5 to under 30, the mortality hazard was about one-fifth higher; at 30 to under 35, almost one-half higher; and at 35 to under 40, almost twice as high. In East Asian cohorts, each five-unit increase above a BMI of 25 was associated with a hazard about 40% higher. Losing weight does not guarantee the same reduction in your own risk.
- Benefit: An individual-participant-data meta-analysis of 239 prospective studies restricted its main observational analysis to never-smokers without chronic disease at baseline who survived the first 5 years of follow-up. All-cause mortality was lowest at BMI 20–25. HRs were 1.07 for BMI 25–<27.5, 1.20 for 27.5–<30, 1.45 for 30–<35, 1.94 for 35–<40 and 2.76 for 40–<60. Above BMI 25, the HR per additional 5 kg/m² in East Asia was 1.39.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Global BMI Mortality Collaboration (2016). Body-mass index and all-cause mortality: individual-participant-data meta-analysis of 239 prospective studies in four continents. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(16)30175-1>
- Notes: Disputed—the so-called obesity paradox. Flegal KM et al. (2013), Association of all-cause mortality with overweight and obesity using standard body mass index categories: a systematic review and meta-analysis, JAMA, reported HRs of 0.94 for overweight and 0.95 for grade 1 obesity versus normal weight. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.113905> Both point estimates were slightly lower; the grade 1 obesity difference was not statistically significant. Much of the debate concerns smoking, pre-existing disease and early deaths: serious illness may cause weight loss before diagnosis. The Global BMI analysis still found higher mortality with excess weight after its exclusions. But comparing people at different BMIs does not measure how much an individual’s mortality risk will change after losing weight.

> Editorial clarification: BMI is weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. The reference group for these HRs was BMI 22.5–<25; 20–25 describes the lowest-mortality range, not a personal prescription. East Asia was not uniformly more sensitive than other regions: its estimate per five units matched Europe’s. The claim that randomized evidence is absent needs correcting: a [meta-analysis of weight-loss intervention trials](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.j4849) found lower all-cause mortality, but did not isolate the effect of losing kilograms from changes in diet and activity. Do not mechanically apply this BMI target to children, pregnancy, older adults or people with substantial muscle mass; weight goals need to reflect health and circumstances.

### 29. If you enjoy spicy food, eat chili peppers more than four times a week
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No extra purchase if you already keep chili peppers in the kitchen. The peppers themselves are not free; the study provides no required portion from which to calculate a standard budget.
- In plain language: People who ate chili peppers more than four times a week had an all-cause mortality hazard during follow-up about 23% lower than those who rarely or never ate them. Their cardiovascular mortality hazard was about one-third lower.
- Benefit: An Italian cohort of about 22,800 people had a median follow-up of 8.2 years and 1,236 deaths. Eating chili peppers >4 times a week versus rarely or never was associated with an all-cause mortality HR of 0.77 (95% CI 0.66–0.90) and a cardiovascular mortality HR of 0.66 (0.50–0.86).
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Bonaccio M, Di Castelnuovo A, Costanzo S, et al. (2019). Chili Pepper Consumption and Mortality in Italian Adults. Journal of the American College of Cardiology, 74(25), 3139-3149. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2019.09.068>
- Notes: Disputed. This was an observational study in a Mediterranean population. Chili eaters may have differed in their overall diet and habits. If spicy food aggravates symptoms of gastroesophageal reflux, hemorrhoids or irritable bowel syndrome, do not force yourself to eat it.

> Editorial clarification: The association persisted after statistical adjustment for adherence to a Mediterranean diet, though residual confounding remains possible. The category was more than four times a week, not a proven therapeutic dose. The study does not establish that adding chili peppers itself extends life. The listed symptoms do not mean that spicy food necessarily worsens everyone’s condition.

### 30. Have one or two servings of milk or yogurt a day if you tolerate them well
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Retail examples for 250–500 mL of milk a day work out to about 0.23–0.46 USD or 0.24–0.48 EUR. These calculations price milk, not yogurt; the volume is for budgeting rather than an exact reproduction of the study serving. [Products, dates and calculation](cost_examples.md).
- In plain language: People who consumed more than two servings of dairy a day had an all-cause mortality hazard during follow-up about 17% lower than people who consumed none. This result concerns total dairy intake, not a separate demonstration of the same effect from two glasses of milk or servings of yogurt.
- Benefit: The PURE cohort included about 136,000 participants in 21 countries, with 9.1 years of follow-up and 6,796 deaths. For >2 servings of dairy a day versus none, the all-cause mortality HR was 0.83 (95% CI 0.72–0.96; P for trend = 0.0052).
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Dehghan M, Mente A, Rangarajan S, et al. (2018). Association of dairy intake with cardiovascular disease and mortality in 21 countries from five continents (PURE): a prospective cohort study. Lancet, 392(10161), 2288-2297. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31812-9>
- Notes: Disputed. PURE included many participants from low- and middle-income countries, where being able to buy dairy daily may reflect greater wealth. Such confounding is hard to eliminate completely. If you are lactose intolerant, try a yogurt you tolerate or lactose-reduced milk; do not force yourself to drink milk.

> Editorial clarification: The study used 244 g as a serving of milk or yogurt. The 0.83 estimate concerns total dairy intake and more than two servings; it cannot be assigned to exactly one or two servings in the heading. This is an observational association, not a guaranteed benefit from adding milk. [NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/lactose-intolerance/eating-diet-nutrition) describes dietary options for lactose intolerance; tolerance depends on the product and amount.

### 31. You do not need to give up eggs, but avoid making three or four a daily habit
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No extra purchase is required. Replacing some eggs with other foods may change your food bill.
- In plain language: In a large US cohort, each additional half of a whole egg per day was associated with an all-cause mortality hazard about 7% higher. Modeled replacements of that half egg with egg whites, fish, poultry or nuts were associated with lower mortality. This was not a food-substitution experiment. The advice does not call for giving up eggs altogether, just avoiding a daily habit of three or four.
- Benefit: A US cohort of about 521,000 people recorded about 129,000 deaths. Each additional half of a whole egg per day was associated with an all-cause mortality HR of 1.07 (95% CI 1.06–1.08). Models replacing half a whole egg with an equal weight of egg whites or egg substitutes, poultry, fish, dairy, nuts or legumes produced lower estimates of all-cause, cardiovascular, cancer and respiratory mortality; the strength and statistical certainty of the associations varied by replacement food and cause of death.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Zhuang P, Wu F, Mao L, et al. (2021). Egg and cholesterol consumption and mortality from cardiovascular and different causes in the United States: A population-based cohort study. PLoS Medicine, 18(2), e1003508. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1003508>
- Notes: Highly disputed. Other large cohorts and meta-analyses have found no association between egg consumption and all-cause mortality. The US Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2015–2020 no longer included the previous numerical daily cholesterol limit. That did not mean dietary cholesterol had ceased to matter. This advice discourages three or four eggs every day; one egg by itself is not a reason to worry.

> Editorial clarification: Food substitutions were statistical, not assigned to participants; the associations do not prove a substitution effect. “Three or four” is not an established safety threshold. Follow any individual dietary advice you have been given. The historical change is explained in the [2015–2020 US guidelines, page 32](https://health.gov/dietaryguidelines/2015/resources/2015-2020_Dietary_Guidelines.pdf); it is not permission to consume eggs or cholesterol without limit.

### 32. If you have a tub and bathing suits your health, sometimes take a bath instead of a shower
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=中 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Water, heating and preparation time. You might set aside about half an hour for the whole process, but do not spend all of it soaking in hot water. Spending depends on bath volume and your utility rates; [calculate it in USD or EUR](cost_examples.md).
- In plain language: In a Japanese study, people who bathed almost daily had a hazard of new cardiovascular events about 28% lower than people who bathed no more than twice a week. For intracerebral hemorrhage, the difference was about 46%. This is an association, not proof that baths prevent disease.
- Benefit: A Japanese cohort of about 30,000 people followed for up to 19 years recorded 2,097 cardiovascular events. For almost-daily bathing versus ≤2 times a week, HRs were 0.72 (95% CI 0.62–0.84) for all cardiovascular events, 0.65 (0.45–0.94) for coronary heart disease, 0.74 (0.62–0.87) for stroke and 0.54 (0.40–0.73) for intracerebral hemorrhage.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Ukai T, Iso H, Yamagishi K, et al. (2020). Habitual tub bathing and risks of incident coronary heart disease and stroke. Heart, 106(10), 732-737. <https://doi.org/10.1136/heartjnl-2019-315752>
- Notes: Disputed. This result comes from Japan and its bathing culture. Many households in China cannot take a bath every day; elsewhere, housing and costs also affect access. Excessively hot water and prolonged soaking can be dangerous, especially for older adults and people with cardiovascular disease. Japan records many fatal bathing accidents involving older adults each year.

> Editorial clarification: The study examined new disease events, not a reduction in all-cause mortality; bathing was not randomly assigned. Half an hour in hot water is not an established safe recommendation. [Japan’s Consumer Affairs Agency](https://www.caa.go.jp/policies/policy/consumer_safety/caution/caution_009/) advises older adults to use water no hotter than 41 °C, limit soaking to about 10 minutes, stand up slowly and tell someone at home before bathing. These are upper safety guides, not targets or guarantees; bathing may be unsuitable when you feel unwell or have health restrictions.
