# 14. Account and information security

Your money and personal information are both at stake. Someone who gains control of an account may take money, contact people in your address book while posing as you, or misuse your identity. Protect your email first: it is often the route to resetting passwords elsewhere.

### 1. Add a second login check to email, payment and social accounts
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Usually free; each account takes a few minutes to set up.
- In plain language: A password alone is not enough. Confirm new sign-ins on a device you control. Choose a passkey or security key if the service offers one. An app prompt helps too, provided you approve only sign-ins you started. If no stronger option is available, turn on an SMS code. Secure email first, then banking, payment and social accounts.
- Benefit: In a Google study of more than 350,000 real account-hijacking attempts, device-based challenges, including phone prompts and security keys, blocked more than 94% of phishing-based attempts and 100% of automated attempts in the study sample. Some knowledge questions, such as a previous login location or recovery address, blocked as few as 10% of phishing-based attempts and 73% of automated ones. These are observations from particular Google challenges, not guarantees for every account. US CISA ranks phishing-resistant keys above ordinary app prompts and treats SMS as a fallback.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Doerfler P, et al. (2019), WWW ’19 conference paper, [Evaluating Login Challenges as a Defense Against Account Takeover](https://doi.org/10.1145/3308558.3313481); [Google Research summary](https://research.google/pubs/evaluating-login-challenges-as-a-defense-against-account-takeover/); [CISA, comparing multifactor methods](https://www.cisa.gov/audiences/small-and-medium-businesses/secure-your-business/require-multifactor-authentication).
- Notes: There is a usability cost. In the same study, 52% of legitimate users failed their first sign-in attempt, but 97% eventually got in. Set up a recovery method and keep backup codes somewhere other than the phone. Decline an unexpected approval prompt: someone else may be trying to log in as you. The study measured Google's challenges, not every app's protection.

### 2. Give your email a unique password you use nowhere else
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Creating a new password is free. A password manager can remember it for you; changing a habit of reusing one password takes effort.
- In plain language: If a password leaks from another site, attackers may try it on your email. Once inside your inbox, they can request password resets for other accounts. Create a long, unique email password and save it in a password manager. Use different passwords everywhere else.
- Benefit: Trying leaked username-and-password pairs on other services is called credential stuffing. A unique password prevents a breach elsewhere from directly giving an attacker your email login. CISA advises a different strong password for each account, at least 16 characters long, stored in a password manager.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: [CISA, use strong passwords](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world/use-strong-passwords); [CISA, choosing a password manager](https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/training/cyb3rsmrt-use-password-manager-create-and-remember-strong-passwords).
- Notes: A browser's built-in password manager is preferable to reusing the same password everywhere. Secure the manager itself with a strong master password and an additional login check. Do not store passwords in plain notes or saved chat messages. Grade C is retained for the unmeasured size of the benefit in this particular entry.

### 3. Lock your phone screen and set a PIN for its SIM
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Both settings are free and usually need to be done once. Find out how to obtain the carrier's PUK before changing the SIM PIN.
- In plain language: A screen lock protects data on the phone. A PIN on a removable SIM makes it harder for someone to move the card into another phone, receive your text codes and reset your accounts. Check both settings, especially if email or banking recovery still relies on that number.
- Benefit: After a phone is lost, a screen lock and SIM PIN address different paths of access: the device itself and SMS delivered after moving its SIM. A SIM PIN does not stop every form of number takeover or account compromise, so also enable additional login checks.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Practical guidance; no separate outcome study supports a quantified effect. [CISA, locking mobile devices](https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/2025-01/Secure-Our-World-Online-Safety-for-Older-Adults-Tip-Sheet.pdf).
- Notes: Look for “SIM lock” in your phone settings. A default PIN may be 1234 or 0000, but confirm it with your carrier rather than guessing. Three wrong attempts usually require a PUK to unlock the SIM. Store your new PIN and the PUK safely. eSIMs and some devices use different procedures.

### 4. If your phone disappears, block the number and device, then secure accounts and payments
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Most steps are free. They take time, and access to another safe phone or computer helps you act quickly.
- In plain language: First ask the carrier to suspend the SIM or eSIM so text codes stop reaching the lost phone. Then put the device in lost mode and lock it remotely; erase it if appropriate and available. From a trusted device, change your email and payment passwords and sign out other sessions. Report a theft to police and keep the report number. Contact your bank immediately if someone may access your money or you see unknown payments: freeze the card or transactions without waiting for the police report.
- Benefit: These actions shorten the window in which someone else can use the number, phone or accounts. The US FCC advises remotely locking a phone even if it might simply be misplaced; after a theft it advises reporting the model and IMEI to police and notifying the carrier promptly. No measured benefit in minutes or percentages establishes this exact order as universally optimal.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: [US FCC, protecting a lost or stolen mobile device](https://www.fcc.gov/consumers/guides/protect-your-mobile-device); [FCC's detailed lost-device advice](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-313511A1.pdf). The sequence is practical advice; for identity-document replacement see [chapter 7](07_when_money_is_tight.md), and for a loan taken in your name see [chapter 8](08_protect_your_rights_and_property.md).
- Notes: In China, the customer-service numbers for three major carriers are 10086, 10010 and 10000. They are not US or European emergency or carrier numbers. Save your own carrier's contact details and your phone number before you need them. You can borrow a phone to call the carrier; avoid entering passwords on an untrusted device. Keep a record of the IMEI away from the phone.

### 5. Freeze your card and dispute a charge you did not authorize
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Reporting a transaction and making a dispute usually require no purchase. Keep the case number, transaction notices and statements. If the physical card is still with you, record where it was at the time.
- In plain language: As soon as you see an unfamiliar payment, contact the bank using its official number and freeze the card or access. Then dispute the transaction in writing. Keep the reference number, bank notices and statement; report an apparent crime to police. Be accurate about whether you authorized a transfer after being tricked: rules for a truly unauthorized payment may differ, but the bank still needs to hear from you immediately.
- Benefit: In China, Supreme People's Court rules effective from 25 May 2021 divide the burden of proof. A cardholder alleging a cloned-card or online theft can use evidence such as the real card's location, the payment location, transaction notices, account records, a police report and the bank notification. A bank or non-bank payment provider claiming the cardholder made or authorized the payment must prove that claim. If the bank fails to preserve available records after notification and the evidence is consequently unavailable, it bears the consequence. Once theft is established, a debit-card customer can seek the stolen deposit with interest and losses; a credit-card customer can seek reversal of the associated borrowing, interest and penalties, compensation and correction of a resulting negative credit record. These are Chinese rules. In the US, federal debit-card protections depend on prompt notice: after a lost or stolen card, reporting within two business days generally limits liability to no more than $50, while later reporting can increase it; statement errors also have a 60-day reporting rule. In the EU, payment-services rules require prompt refund of unauthorized transactions subject to exceptions and can leave a payer liable for up to EUR 50 in some lost-instrument cases. Neither regime should be confused with the Chinese court rule.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: [China's Supreme People's Court, bank-card dispute rules, 2021, Articles 4, 5, 7, 14 and 15](https://www.court.gov.cn/fabu/xiangqing/304771.html); [US CFPB, unauthorized electronic transfers](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1005/6/); [EU Payment Services Directive, Articles 72–74](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/ALL/?uri=CELEX%3A02015L2366-20240408).
- Notes: Under the Chinese rules, fault in safeguarding a card, password or verification code can reduce recovery to the extent of that fault. Losses increased by delay in reporting a missing card can remain with its holder. A payment provider's specific, clear promise of advance reimbursement may be enforceable. Use the first minutes to stop further transactions rather than argue about liability. If you sent money yourself because someone deceived you, follow the separate steps in [chapter 8, item 2](08_protect_your_rights_and_property.md). In China, 110 or 96110 can be used to report an urgent fraudulent transfer; in the US or Europe, contact your own bank and local police. See item 1 for securing verification codes. US credit cards and national EU procedures have additional rules, so ask the issuer which dispute process applies.

### 6. Periodically review signed-in devices and connected apps
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Reviewing the lists is free and takes a few minutes when the service provides them. You need to remember to check.
- In plain language: Open the security settings for email, payment and social accounts. Review phones and computers with active sessions and outside apps allowed to use the account. Remove devices you do not recognize and apps you no longer use. End unwanted sessions and change the password if you find evidence of an unfamiliar login.
- Benefit: Session and permission lists can reveal access before someone sends messages or moves money. An attacker may remain in an account for a while before acting. No study supplies a reliable percentage reduction in losses from this particular habit.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Practical guidance; no separate outcome study supports a quantified effect.
- Notes: The setting may be named “Devices”, “Sessions”, “Security” or “Connected apps”. If you find an unfamiliar device, sign out all sessions, change the password and check recovery methods. An empty list of unfamiliar devices is not proof the account was never accessed.

### 7. Decline app permissions that are unnecessary for the service you want
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=自由 -->
- Cost: No purchase is needed. Read each permission request and choose “While using the app” or “Allow once” where offered instead of accepting everything.
- In plain language: A map may need your location to show directions; a flashlight does not need your address book to shine. Decline an apparently unrelated permission and see whether the function still works. If information really is necessary, the provider should explain why. What counts as necessary depends on the service and the law that applies.
- Benefit: In China, Personal Information Protection Law Article 6 limits collection to the minimum necessary for the processing purpose. Article 16 says a provider cannot deny a product or service merely because someone refuses or withdraws consent, except where the information is necessary to supply it; Article 15 permits withdrawal of consent. In the EU, GDPR data minimization similarly limits collection to what is necessary, and consent must be withdrawable, but this is not an unlimited right to use every service without supplying required information. US privacy rules vary by state and sector; the Chinese Article 16 does not apply across the US.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: [China's Personal Information Protection Law, 2021, Articles 6, 15 and 16](http://www.npc.gov.cn/npc/c2/c30834/202108/t20210820_313088.html); [European Commission, GDPR data-minimization principle](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/information-business-and-organisations/principles-gdpr_en); [European Commission, withdrawing consent](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/information-business-and-organisations/legal-grounds-processing-data_en).
- Notes: After installing an app, review its permissions in your device settings and switch off unnecessary access. You can grant a permission once later if the feature needs it. If a service insists on data that seems unrelated, ask why and keep the response. A map may need location for navigation, but whether continuous access is necessary is a separate question.

### 8. Ask to see, correct or erase personal data held about you
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=自由 -->
- Cost: Making a request is usually free. A complaint or court case takes time; legal fees depend on the country and the case.
- In plain language: Write to the organization holding your information. Specify what you want to see, correct or erase, and keep the request and response. Withdrawing consent or closing an account does not always mean every record can be erased immediately: another legal basis may require some records to be kept. Ask for the reason if your request is refused.
- Benefit: In China, Article 45 of the Personal Information Protection Law allows access and a copy, while Article 46 covers correction. Article 47 lists five erasure grounds, including achieving the purpose, ending the service or storage period, and withdrawal of consent. If the organization fails to erase qualifying data itself, the person may demand erasure. Under Article 50, refusal to exercise a right must be explained and can be challenged in court. In the EU, the GDPR provides rights of access, rectification and erasure in defined circumstances; a controller that refuses must explain why and identify complaint and judicial remedies. The US has no single equivalent right for every service and every state: check the applicable state or sector rule.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: [China's Personal Information Protection Law, 2021, Articles 45–47 and 50](http://www.npc.gov.cn/npc/c2/c30834/202108/t20210820_313088.html); [European Commission, access, correction and erasure rights](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/information-individuals_en); [European Commission, handling refusals and complaints](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/information-business-and-organisations/dealing-requests-individuals_en).
- Notes: Closing an account and erasing all personal data are separate requests. In the EU, erasure has exceptions, for example where records must be kept by law or for legal claims. Before selling an old phone, sign out, unlink it and then wipe it. Legal rights cannot recall copies already obtained by someone else.

### 9. Ask for another way to verify yourself when a service wants a face scan
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Asking is free. A refusal may mean spending time on a written request or complaint.
- In plain language: You can change a password after a leak, but not your face. Before agreeing to a scan, ask why it is needed, where the template is stored and whether a card, document or password would work. In China, if another method achieves the same purpose, face recognition cannot be the only option. Elsewhere, check the local rule and the particular service rather than assuming the Chinese rule applies.
- Benefit: China's face-recognition rules, effective from 1 June 2025, require a reasonable and convenient non-biometric alternative under Article 10 when one achieves the same purpose; Article 12 forbids misleading or coercing people into a scan to receive a service. Where processing relies on consent, Article 6 calls for separate, informed and voluntary consent and a way to withdraw it; for children under 14, Article 7 requires parental or guardian consent. Article 13 limits public-place face-recognition installations to what is necessary for public safety, with prominent notice, and bans them inside hotel rooms, public bathhouses, changing rooms and toilets. Under Article 8, facial data normally must stay on the recognition device rather than be sent over the internet, unless a separate consent or legal exception applies. In the EU, biometric identification data has special protection under the GDPR; European data-protection guidance says that when consent is required for biometric authentication, access to a service should not depend on agreeing to the scan and an alternative should be offered. US rules differ by state; an FTC best-practice report is guidance, not a nationwide legal entitlement to an alternative.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: [China's face-recognition safety rules, Order No. 19 of 2025, Articles 6–8, 10, 12, 13 and 15; effective 1 June 2025](https://www.cac.gov.cn/2025-03/21/c_1744174262156096.htm); [European Data Protection Board, guidance on video and facial recognition](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/edpb_guidelines_201903_videosurveillance.pdf); [US FTC, facial-recognition best practices](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2012/10/ftc-recommends-best-practices-companies-use-facial-recognition-technologies).
- Notes: In China, an organization storing facial data for 100,000 people must register with the provincial or higher cybersecurity authority within 30 working days after reaching that threshold. Registration is an obligation on the organization, not proof that its database is safe. Ask for a written explanation if an alternative is refused and use the appropriate regulator's complaint route. Special law may govern some financial or public services. See item 8 for access and erasure requests.
