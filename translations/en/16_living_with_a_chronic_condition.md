# 16. Living with a chronic condition

The stakes are overall mortality and money. A diagnosis is not the end of the story: what matters is how you manage the condition over the decades that follow.

### 1. Take prescribed medicines as directed, even when you feel better
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Following an existing prescription has no separate fee, but the medicine itself may cost money; insurance coverage and prices differ across the US and Europe. Daily doses take a few minutes. The hard part is keeping up the routine. Avoiding complications may save money, but savings are not guaranteed.
- In plain language: Feeling better does not automatically mean treatment is finished. Follow the prescribed schedule; if side effects, cost or a change of dose are a problem, speak with your clinician or pharmacist. Stopping certain blood-pressure medicines, anticoagulants or anti-rejection medicines on your own can be dangerous. People who regularly took medicine died less often in the pooled studies, but regular takers of **placebo** also died less often. That makes the apparent difference an unreliable estimate of what the medicine-taking habit alone caused.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis combines results from several studies. This one covered 21 studies and 46,847 people and found an association between good adherence and lower **odds** of death during follow-up: odds ratio 0.56 versus poor adherence (95% confidence interval 0.50–0.63), about 44% lower odds. The confidence interval expresses uncertainty around that estimate. For treatments with established benefit, the odds ratio was 0.55 (0.49–0.62), about 45% lower odds. These observational comparisons do not prove an equally large reduction in individual mortality risk caused by adherence alone.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: [Simpson et al., BMJ (2006), 333(7557):15, meta-analysis of adherence and mortality](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.38875.675486.55); [NHS guidance on not stopping rivaroxaban without medical advice](https://www.nhs.uk/medicines/rivaroxaban/); [NHS England guide to blood-pressure medicine and treatment changes](https://www.england.nhs.uk/north-west/wp-content/uploads/sites/48/2026/01/high_blood_pressure_and_stroke_guide.pdf).
- Notes: In the same meta-analysis, good adherence to **placebo** was also associated with lower odds of death: 0.56 (0.43–0.74). The researchers call this the “healthy adherer” effect: people who keep up a dosing routine may also take better care of their health in other ways. The 0.56 figure is therefore not a causal effect of every prescription. Some medicines should be reduced, replaced or stopped when a clinician advises it; do not assume treatment must continue forever. Seek urgent medical care for a serious adverse reaction.

### 2. Check chronic-care eligibility and payment arrangements before treatment away from home
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Checking coverage before travel need not carry a separate fee; care, medicines and transport may still cost money. In China, the cross-province registration can be made through the national medical-insurance app. The US and European systems have different rules.
- In plain language: In China, eligible outpatient care for several chronic or serious conditions can be billed directly to medical insurance in another province. First have your special outpatient benefit confirmed where you are insured, then register for cross-province care and **check that the clinic supports direct billing for your condition**. Otherwise you may have to pay first and claim reimbursement later. In the US, check your plan's provider network and out-of-area rules. In Europe, check the rules for your country and destination before relying on an EHIC or arranging planned treatment. China's registration process does not apply elsewhere.
- Benefit: China's National Healthcare Security Administration reported that cross-province direct settlement for 5 outpatient categories had become broadly available at county level: hypertension, diabetes, outpatient radio- or chemotherapy for malignant tumours, dialysis for uraemia, and anti-rejection treatment after organ transplant. A 2024 notice provided for 5 more categories in addition to those original 5. Direct billing avoids paying the full bill upfront **only where** benefit eligibility, travel registration and the specific clinic's participation are confirmed; the patient's share still depends on their home insurance rules.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: [China's National Healthcare Security Administration and Ministry of Finance, Notice No. 19 (2024)](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202409/content_6974467.htm); [official NHSA notice page, 2024](https://www.nhsa.gov.cn/art/2024/9/13/art_104_13861.html); [NHSA guide to checking eligibility and participating clinics](https://www.nhsa.gov.cn/art/2022/11/26/art_114_9857.html); [HealthCare.gov on US provider networks](https://www.healthcare.gov/choose-a-plan/plan-types/); [European Commission on care for chronic conditions during a temporary stay and planned treatment](https://employment-social-affairs.ec.europa.eu/policies-and-activities/moving-working-europe/eu-social-security-coordination/planned-medical-treatment_en).
- Notes: In China, obtain the special outpatient benefit **where you are insured** before registering for care in another province. Check current local information for the 5 added categories and for participating clinics. Registration alone does not guarantee direct settlement. The NHSA says that where a clinic does not offer it, patients may need to pay and then seek reimbursement under their home area's rules; settling at the ordinary outpatient rate without checking may reduce reimbursement. In the US, non-emergency care outside a plan's network may cost more or be uncovered, depending on the plan. An EHIC can cover medically necessary care for a chronic condition during an eligible temporary stay in Europe; planned care has different rules and may require advance authorisation. Neither system promises China's form of direct settlement.

### 3. Keep follow-up appointments at the intervals your clinician recommends, and keep your results together
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Appointments, tests and travel take time; what you pay depends on the test and your coverage. For a concrete example, a self-pay complete blood count was listed at $29 by Labcorp OnDemand in the US and €24 by BloedCheckup in the Netherlands in September 2026. Those are prices for **one example test**, not the cost of every follow-up; collection or consultation fees may be extra. You also need to save the results each time.
- In plain language: With a chronic condition, the trend may matter more than one result. Keep dates, measurements, medicines and doses together in a notebook or a secure folder on your phone. When you change clinics, bring the records and actual reports so the new clinician can see what has already been done and decide what needs repeating.
- Benefit: A series of results helps you and your clinician discuss how the condition is changing. If an earlier test can be used, you may avoid paying for and travelling to a duplicate test; sometimes a repeat is medically necessary. Keeping a record by itself has not been shown here to reduce mortality or guarantee savings.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Author's practical experience; no direct study of this specific advice; [MedlinePlus on personal health records](https://medlineplus.gov/personalhealthrecords.html); [MedlinePlus on interpreting test results with your clinician](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/how-to-understand-your-lab-results/); [Labcorp OnDemand complete blood count price](https://www.ondemand.labcorp.com/lab-tests/complete-blood-count); [BloedCheckup complete blood count price](https://bloedcheckup.nl/en/onderzoek/bloedbeeld-uitgebreid).
- Notes: Record at least four things: date, result, medicine taken and dose. A photo of the report can help if the units and reference range are visible. Your clinician sets the testing interval and decides whether a test should be repeated; do not change treatment based on a single result without discussing it.

### 4. Do not stop prescribed treatment to try a folk remedy or supplement
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Skipping an unproven product avoids its purchase price and takes little time. The harder part can be resisting repeated claims from adverts or people you know that it should replace your medicines.
- In plain language: Do not swap a treatment with established benefit for a product that has not been shown to treat your condition. If you want to take a supplement **alongside** your medicines, first tell your clinician or pharmacist its exact name and ingredients. Some combinations are dangerous: St John's wort can weaken medicines used after an organ transplant, while ginkgo taken with warfarin may raise the risk of bleeding.
- Benefit: You keep receiving prescribed treatment and reduce the risk of a harmful interaction. No specific gain in life expectancy from refusing supplements is calculated here; decide with your clinician if a medicine needs to be changed or stopped. [Chapter 6](06_what_to_skip.md) also covers products without established benefit.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Author's practical experience; no direct study of this specific advice; related examples appear in [chapter 6](06_what_to_skip.md); [NCCIH on avoiding unproven substitutes for medical treatment](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/are-you-considering-a-complementary-health-approach); [FDA on supplement–medicine interactions, including St John's wort and warfarin](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/mixing-medications-and-dietary-supplements-can-endanger-your-health).
- Notes: “Natural” does not mean safe. Show the packaging or ingredient list to a professional, especially if you take anticoagulants, anti-rejection drugs or medicines with a narrow safety margin. Do not stop a prescribed medicine on your own because of a supplement; seek urgent medical care for a serious adverse reaction.

### 5. If your condition is stable, ask whether a longer prescription is suitable
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Asking about a longer prescription has no separate fee, though appointments and medicines may cost money. The question takes little time. Fewer visits are possible only if your clinician agrees that a longer supply is appropriate and the medicine is available.
- In plain language: In China, a primary-care facility may prescribe **up to 12 weeks of medicine at one time** for an eligible person with a chronic condition. This is meant for a confirmed condition and a stable treatment plan; a clinician decides whether it fits. If you travel to a large hospital each month only to collect the same medicine, ask the local clinic about a longer prescription and local supply. Elsewhere the limits differ: some US Medicare drug plans offer a 2- or 3-month supply of covered medicines, while in the UK a repeat prescription often lets you order regular medicines without seeing a GP every time. Neither is a universal 12-week entitlement.
- Benefit: A longer prescription may spare a monthly journey, queue, appointment and any appointment fee charged just to renew the same medicine — sometimes half a day per trip. A separate part of China's 2026 policy concerns **hospital inpatient care**: within one pooled insurance region, reimbursement rates at successive facility levels should in principle differ by about **10 percentage points**. That is a policy direction for local inpatient rules, not a 10% discount on an outpatient prescription.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: [General Office of China's State Council, measures on tiered medical care (2026)](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202604/content_7065031.htm); [full text: the 12-week prescription ceiling and approximately 10-percentage-point principle for inpatient reimbursement](https://www.nhsa.gov.cn/art/2026/4/10/art_14_20158.html); [US Medicare on 2- or 3-month pharmacy supplies and mail order](https://www.medicare.gov/health-drug-plans/part-d/what-drug-plans-cover/pharmacies); [NHS guidance on repeat prescriptions](https://www.nhs.uk/nhs-services/prescriptions/how-to-order-a-repeat-prescription/).
- Notes: “Eligible” matters. Your clinician checks the diagnosis, treatment stability and the particular medicine. Even in China, 12 weeks is a ceiling, not a guaranteed supply for everyone. If the local clinic does not stock your medicine, ask whether it records shortages and can arrange delivery. A longer prescription does not replace clinical reviews. Check your own plan or local health service for supply limits and costs; do not change a dose to make medicine last until the next refill.

### 6. Before signing up with a family doctor, ask what is covered and what you must pay for
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Asking for the terms before signing or buying an extra service package has no separate fee and takes a few minutes. Any price depends on the services and local coverage; there is no single US or European rate.
- In plain language: In China, a family-doctor agreement may offer a basic and a personalised service package. **Eligible services within the basic package** can be paid by medical insurance under its rules; the entire package is not automatically free. The patient pays for a personalised package, which the clinic must file with the county health authority. If someone offers an “upgrade”, ask what each package contains, which services insurance will pay for, whether the paid package has been filed and its annual price.
- Benefit: Checking first helps you see your own costs and choose services you will actually use. China's policy provides for insurance payment for the basic package **according to the rules**, while the personalised package is self-pay and must be filed with the county health authority. In the US, use your plan's Summary of Benefits and Coverage to check primary-care charges and included services. Across Europe, entitlements and out-of-pocket costs depend on the country. China's two-package arrangement does not govern either system.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: [General Office of China's State Council, measures on tiered medical care (2026)](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202604/content_7065031.htm); [full text published by China's National Healthcare Security Administration](https://www.nhsa.gov.cn/art/2026/4/10/art_14_20158.html); [NHSA explanation of which basic-package services may be insured](https://www.nhsa.gov.cn/art/2026/3/16/art_105_19933.html); [HealthCare.gov on the Summary of Benefits and Coverage](https://www.healthcare.gov/health-care-law-protections/summary-of-benefits-and-coverage/); [Your Europe on differences in national healthcare coverage](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/health/when-living-abroad/health-insurance-cover/index_en.htm).
- Notes: The value of a regular clinician is continuing care for your condition, not the number of items in a sales package. Before paying, ask three questions: is this the basic or personalised package, has the paid package been filed with the county health authority, and what does it cost per year? Those package names and the filing rule apply to China. Even there, inclusion in a basic package does not make every service free for every patient.

### 7. Have your eyes and feet checked on the schedule for your type of diabetes
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: An eye examination and travel take time; the harder part is remembering the next appointment when neither your eyes nor feet hurt. For a sense of private-pay prices in September 2026, a Georgia, US ophthalmology clinic listed **$250** for an initial comprehensive visit including dilation unless contraindicated, while a Croatian clinic listed **€90** for an ophthalmological examination with dilated pupils and tonometry. These are examples of specific services, not standard diabetes-screening fees. Insurance or public coverage and any charge for the foot examination depend on where you receive care.
- In plain language: Diabetes can damage the retina and the nerves in your feet before you notice symptoms. With **type 2 diabetes**, book a comprehensive dilated eye examination when you are diagnosed. For an **adult with type 1 diabetes**, the first such examination is recommended **5 years after the disease begins**. Agree on later intervals with your eye-care professional. Have a clinician examine your feet at least once a year, even if they feel fine.
- Benefit: Finding diabetic retinopathy and foot problems early gives clinicians a chance to treat them before vision loss, ulcers or amputation. After one or more annual eye examinations show no retinopathy **and** glucose measures are within your target range, screening every **1–2 years** may be considered. If retinopathy is present, dilated examinations are generally needed at least annually, and more often if it progresses. A comprehensive foot examination every year is a minimum; if you have lost protective sensation or have had an ulcer, your feet should be checked at every visit.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: [American Diabetes Association (2026), Standards of Care in Diabetes—2026, section 12, Retinopathy, Neuropathy, and Foot Care: recommendation 12.3 — first eye examination for adults with type 1 diabetes after 5 years; 12.4 — type 2 at diagnosis; 12.5 — 1–2 years if earlier examinations are normal and glucose is at target; 12.23 — comprehensive foot evaluation at least annually](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc26-S012); [Gwinnett Ophthalmology, Georgia: self-pay comprehensive eye examination price](https://gwinnettophthalmology.com/self-pay/); [Polyclinic Dr Balog, Croatia: dilated examination price](https://poliklinika-balog.hr/en/prices/).
- Notes: Type 2 diabetes can have raised glucose for years before it is diagnosed, which is why the first eye examination for type 2 is due at diagnosis. For an adult with type 1 diabetes, the first routine eye examination is recommended 5 years after onset. The **1–2-year** interval is an option only after normal examinations and glucose results within target; your clinician may advise a different schedule. If feeling in your feet is reduced, inspect the soles daily with an unbreakable mirror or ask someone to help. Show a wound, redness or swelling to a clinician promptly rather than waiting for the annual visit. The ADA grades the timing of eye examinations B and the annual comprehensive foot evaluation A; an overall A label for this item does not give every subrecommendation the same evidence grade.

### 8. After a kidney stone, drink enough water and keep salt to 6 g a day or less
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: You do not need a special product; ordinary safe drinking water may have a small cost. Spread drinks through the day and keep the habit going. More fluid may be needed when you sweat heavily or work in heat. If a clinician has limited your fluids because of heart or kidney disease, ask for an individual target rather than following a general volume.
- In plain language: A stone can come back after it passes or is removed. In a five-year trial involving 199 people after a **first unexplained calcium stone**, another stone appeared in 12.1% of those advised to drink enough water to produce at least **2 litres of urine a day**, compared with 27.0% of those given no preventive treatment. The trial target was **urine output**, not a fixed 2-litre amount to drink. For prevention, the UK's NICE guideline advises adults to drink **2.5–3 litres of water a day** and keep salt to **no more than 6 g a day**. Agree on your own fluid target if you have another condition that affects how much you can safely drink.
- Benefit: In this **5-year randomised trial**, **199** people after a first idiopathic calcium stone were assigned to **2 groups**: one was advised to drink enough water to produce at least **2 litres of urine in 24 hours**, and the other received no preventive treatment. Another stone occurred over **5 years** in 12 of 99 people in the higher-water group and 27 of 100 in the control group (12.1% versus 27.0%; **P = 0.008**). That is 14.9 percentage points fewer recurrences, or about 55% lower **relative** recurrence in this group; it does not measure deaths. The average time until recurrence was **38.7 ± 13.2 months** versus **25.1 ± 16.4 months** (**P = 0.016**). Before treatment, 24-hour urine volume was lower in men with calcium stones than in men without stones (**1,057 ± 238 mL** versus **1,401 ± 562 mL; P < 0.0001**) and lower in women with stones than in women without stones (**990 ± 230 mL** versus **1,239 ± 440 mL; P < 0.001**). A P value describes how incompatible the observed difference is with no effect under the statistical test; it is not a personal probability of benefit. **NICE guideline NG118, recommendation 1.8.1**, advises adults to drink **2.5–3 litres of water a day**; children and young people, **1–2 litres depending on age**. It also advises adding fresh lemon juice to water, avoiding carbonated drinks, and limiting adult salt to 6 g daily.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: [Borghi et al. (1996), *The Journal of Urology*, 155(3):839–843, 5-year randomised trial](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0022-5347(01)66321-3); [NICE guideline NG118 (2019), recommendation 1.8.1 on fluid, lemon juice, carbonated drinks, salt and calcium](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng118/chapter/Recommendations); [European Association of Urology, guideline on stone recurrence prevention and 24-hour urine targets](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/urolithiasis/chapter/metabolic-evaluation-and-recurrence-prevention); [CARI kidney-stone nutrition guideline on the trial's urine-output target and individual fluid limits](https://www.cariguidelines.org/guidelines/kidney-stones/nutrition-therapy-for-the-prevention-of-kidney-stones/).
- Notes: The roughly **55% relative reduction** with no special product to buy makes this the highest-value item in this section under the book's ranking, but it applies to the study population and drinking water can have a cost. The amount you **drink** and the amount of **urine** you produce are different: sweat and heat increase the water you lose. The trial aimed for at least **2 litres of urine a day**; the European urology guideline now advises a 24-hour urine volume **above 2.5 litres** for stone prevention. Do not force this much fluid if you have been given a medical fluid restriction. The trial involved first-time idiopathic calcium stones; uric-acid, infection and cystine stones can need different measures. If stones recur, ask about stone-composition analysis and a **24-hour urine test** before deciding whether medicine or a more specific diet is needed. Do **not** cut out dietary calcium to prevent stones; see [chapter 6, item 21](06_what_to_skip.md). Severe flank or abdominal colic needs medical assessment; fever with a suspected blocked kidney or inability to pass urine is urgent, not a problem to solve by drinking more water. Visible blood in urine without pain also needs assessment; see [chapter 1, item 27](01_avoid_dying_early.md). This trial measured stone recurrence, not mortality.

### 9. If you are prescribed gout treatment, lower urate to the target and keep it there
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: The medicine may be inexpensive, but what you pay depends on the dose, pharmacy and coverage. On 25 September 2026, the US nonprofit [Rx Outreach listed 100 mg allopurinol at $10 for a 30-day supply](https://rxoutreach.org/find-your-medication/); a German pharmacy listed [100 tablets of 100 mg allopurinol at a €13.60 retail list price](https://www.shop-apotheke.com/arzneimittel/3399818/allopurinol-al-100.htm), with a separate €5 statutory copayment shown for eligible prescriptions. These are **different price bases**, not a guaranteed monthly cost for your prescribed dose. While the dose is being adjusted, have urate checked at intervals agreed with your clinician—often every few weeks or monthly—and keep checking after the target is reached. Your clinician will usually also prescribe a medicine to prevent flares for the first **3–6 months**. The hard part is continuing treatment when your joints no longer hurt.
- In plain language: If your clinician prescribes a urate-lowering medicine, take it as directed and use blood tests to guide the dose. The usual goal is **below 360 µmol/L (6 mg/dL)**. Treating pain during a flare does not replace lowering urate over time. In a UK trial of **517 people**, **95%** reached the urate target after **2 years** with education and dose adjustment, compared with **30%** under usual care. Do not stop a successful urate-lowering medicine simply because the number is now normal: urate crystals and the risk of future flares can remain. But **one uncomplicated first flare** does not automatically mean everyone needs lifelong medicine; discuss whether it is indicated for you.
- Benefit: A randomised trial enrolled **517 adults** who had had a flare in the previous **12 months**: **255** received nurse-led education and dose adjustment towards a measured target, while **262** received usual GP care. After **2 years**, serum urate was **<360 µmol/L (<6 mg/dL)** in **95% versus 30%** (**RR 3.18; 95% CI 2.42–4.18; P < 0.0001**). The confidence interval describes uncertainty around the estimate; this result is about reaching a laboratory target, **not mortality**. Flare frequency during year 2, tophi and quality of life also favoured nurse-led care. The economic analysis of the **UK care programme**, not the price of a pill, found **£5,066 per additional quality-adjusted life-year (QALY)**. Later, questionnaires were sent to **438** trial participants and **82%** were returned. For the previous **12 months**, respondents *self-reported* a median of **0 flares (interquartile range 0–0)** after nurse-led care versus **1 (0–3)** after usual care (**P < 0.001**). The interquartile range covers the middle half of responses. The nurse-led group was more likely still to take urate-lowering therapy (**adjusted RR 1.19; 95% CI 1.09–1.30**); this follow-up relied on respondents' reports, not a new clinical count of every flare. The **2020 American College of Rheumatology (ACR)** guideline **strongly** recommends starting urate-lowering therapy with **subcutaneous tophi**, **gout-related joint damage on imaging**, or **at least two flares a year**. After more than one but less frequent flares, the recommendation is conditional. After a first uncomplicated flare, it conditionally recommends against starting, except when **chronic kidney disease is stage 3 or worse**, serum urate is **>9 mg/dL**, or there is **urolithiasis**; individual circumstances matter. Once treatment is started, adjust the dose using repeat serum urate results to reach and maintain **<6 mg/dL (about 360 µmol/L)**. ACR prefers allopurinol first-line, including for people with **stage 3 or worse kidney disease**; start at **≤100 mg/day**, lower with kidney disease, then titrate. It recommends anti-inflammatory flare prophylaxis for at least **3–6 months** on starting treatment. Continuing successful therapy indefinitely is a **conditional**, not absolute, recommendation. In one selected case series of people with years of good control who stopped therapy, only **13% (27 of 211)** of those whose serum urate stayed **<7 mg/dL** remained flare-free over **5 years**. This does not give every patient who stops treatment the same risk. In studies cited by ACR, limiting or avoiding alcohol was associated with serum urate **1.6 mg/dL** lower than without doing so; one unit of beer was associated with an increase of **0.16 mg/dL**. Healthy, Mediterranean and DASH dietary patterns had still smaller effects on serum urate. A healthy diet matters, but where medicine is indicated it does not replace dose adjustment.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: [Doherty et al. (2018), *The Lancet*, 392(10156):1403–1412, randomised trial and UK cost per QALY](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)32158-5); [Fuller et al. (2020), *Rheumatology*, 59(3):575–579, questionnaire follow-up](https://doi.org/10.1093/rheumatology/kez333); [FitzGerald et al. (2020), *Arthritis Care & Research*, 72(6):744–760, ACR gout guideline](https://doi.org/10.1002/acr.24180); [ACR correction (2020) to the wording on populations for genetic testing](https://doi.org/10.1002/acr.24401); [NICE NG219, treatment indications, urate target, monitoring and duration](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng219/chapter/Recommendations); [FDA allopurinol label: rash and flares on starting treatment](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2024/018832s056s058s061%2C018877s063s065s068lbl.pdf); [US price](https://rxoutreach.org/find-your-medication/), [German price and copayment](https://www.shop-apotheke.com/arzneimittel/3399818/allopurinol-al-100.htm).
- Notes: Before starting allopurinol, tell your clinician about a previous drug rash and relevant ancestry. Under the [corrected 2020 ACR wording](https://doi.org/10.1002/acr.24401), testing for **HLA-B\*5801** before treatment is *conditionally* recommended for people from **certain Asian populations**, including Han Chinese, Korean and Thai backgrounds, and for African American patients; it is not recommended universally for everyone else. The allele's reported prevalence is **7.4%** across those three Asian groups, **3.8%** among African Americans, and **0.7%** each among white and Hispanic populations. In the evidence cited by ACR, Asian and African American allopurinol users each had about **3 times** the risk of allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome compared with white users; severe reactions can cause extensive skin peeling and be fatal. **If a new rash appears after starting allopurinol, stop taking it and seek medical attention promptly**—this safety instruction is different from how to handle an ordinary gout flare. The treatment target when therapy is prescribed remains **<360 µmol/L**, but medicine safety takes priority over reaching a number on your own. Flares can become more likely when urate-lowering treatment begins. Your clinician should plan anti-inflammatory prophylaxis for **3–6 months** and tell you how to treat a flare. If an ordinary flare occurs without signs of a dangerous reaction, do not abandon urate-lowering treatment on your own; contact your clinician and follow the flare plan. A high urate result **without any gout flare** is a different question: see [chapter 6, entry 19](06_what_to_skip.md). For sugary drinks and alcohol, see [chapter 2, entries 7 and 20](02_protect_your_health.md). UK NICE guidance also offers urate-lowering therapy for multiple or troublesome flares, stage 3–5 kidney disease, diuretic use, tophi or chronic gouty arthritis, and advises discussing the option after a first or later flare; it says treatment is usually lifelong once started. This entry's studies assessed urate control and flares, not the effect of this care programme on all-cause mortality. The A grade rests chiefly on the randomised care comparison; conditional recommendations and genetic testing do not all have the same certainty of evidence.
