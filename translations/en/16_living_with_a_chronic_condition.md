# 16. Living with a chronic condition

The stakes are overall mortality and money. A diagnosis is not the end of the story: what matters is how you manage the condition over the decades that follow.

### 1. Take prescribed medicines as directed, even when you feel better
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Following an existing prescription has no separate fee, but the medicine itself may cost money; insurance coverage and prices differ across the US and Europe. Daily doses take a few minutes. The hard part is keeping up the routine. Avoiding complications may save money, but savings are not guaranteed.
- In plain language: Feeling better does not automatically mean treatment is finished. Follow the prescribed schedule; if side effects, cost or a change of dose are a problem, speak with your clinician or pharmacist. Stopping certain blood-pressure medicines, anticoagulants or anti-rejection medicines on your own can be dangerous. People who regularly took medicine died less often in the pooled studies, but regular takers of **placebo** also died less often. That makes the apparent difference an unreliable estimate of what the medicine-taking habit alone caused.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis combines results from several studies. This one covered 21 studies and 46,847 people and found an association between good adherence and lower **odds** of death during follow-up: odds ratio 0.56 versus poor adherence (95% confidence interval 0.50–0.63), about 44% lower odds. The confidence interval expresses uncertainty around that estimate. For treatments with established benefit, the odds ratio was 0.55 (0.49–0.62), about 45% lower odds. These observational comparisons do not prove an equally large reduction in individual mortality risk caused by adherence alone.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: [Simpson et al., BMJ (2006), 333(7557):15, meta-analysis of adherence and mortality](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.38875.675486.55); [NHS guidance on not stopping rivaroxaban without medical advice](https://www.nhs.uk/medicines/rivaroxaban/); [NHS England guide to blood-pressure medicine and treatment changes](https://www.england.nhs.uk/north-west/wp-content/uploads/sites/48/2026/01/high_blood_pressure_and_stroke_guide.pdf).
- Notes: In the same meta-analysis, good adherence to **placebo** was also associated with lower odds of death: 0.56 (0.43–0.74). The researchers call this the “healthy adherer” effect: people who keep up a dosing routine may also take better care of their health in other ways. The 0.56 figure is therefore not a causal effect of every prescription. Some medicines should be reduced, replaced or stopped when a clinician advises it; do not assume treatment must continue forever. Seek urgent medical care for a serious adverse reaction.

### 2. Check chronic-care eligibility and payment arrangements before treatment away from home
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Checking coverage before travel need not carry a separate fee; care, medicines and transport may still cost money. In China, the cross-province registration can be made through the national medical-insurance app. The US and European systems have different rules.
- In plain language: In China, eligible outpatient care for several chronic or serious conditions can be billed directly to medical insurance in another province. First have your special outpatient benefit confirmed where you are insured, then register for cross-province care and **check that the clinic supports direct billing for your condition**. Otherwise you may have to pay first and claim reimbursement later. In the US, check your plan's provider network and out-of-area rules. In Europe, check the rules for your country and destination before relying on an EHIC or arranging planned treatment. China's registration process does not apply elsewhere.
- Benefit: China's National Healthcare Security Administration reported that cross-province direct settlement for 5 outpatient categories had become broadly available at county level: hypertension, diabetes, outpatient radio- or chemotherapy for malignant tumours, dialysis for uraemia, and anti-rejection treatment after organ transplant. A 2024 notice provided for 5 more categories in addition to those original 5. Direct billing avoids paying the full bill upfront **only where** benefit eligibility, travel registration and the specific clinic's participation are confirmed; the patient's share still depends on their home insurance rules.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: [China's National Healthcare Security Administration and Ministry of Finance, Notice No. 19 (2024)](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202409/content_6974467.htm); [official NHSA notice page, 2024](https://www.nhsa.gov.cn/art/2024/9/13/art_104_13861.html); [NHSA guide to checking eligibility and participating clinics](https://www.nhsa.gov.cn/art/2022/11/26/art_114_9857.html); [HealthCare.gov on US provider networks](https://www.healthcare.gov/choose-a-plan/plan-types/); [European Commission on care for chronic conditions during a temporary stay and planned treatment](https://employment-social-affairs.ec.europa.eu/policies-and-activities/moving-working-europe/eu-social-security-coordination/planned-medical-treatment_en).
- Notes: In China, obtain the special outpatient benefit **where you are insured** before registering for care in another province. Check current local information for the 5 added categories and for participating clinics. Registration alone does not guarantee direct settlement. The NHSA says that where a clinic does not offer it, patients may need to pay and then seek reimbursement under their home area's rules; settling at the ordinary outpatient rate without checking may reduce reimbursement. In the US, non-emergency care outside a plan's network may cost more or be uncovered, depending on the plan. An EHIC can cover medically necessary care for a chronic condition during an eligible temporary stay in Europe; planned care has different rules and may require advance authorisation. Neither system promises China's form of direct settlement.
