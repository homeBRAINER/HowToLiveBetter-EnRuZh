# 4. Don’t waste time

This chapter counts time alone: how many hours you can free up each day, week, or year, or how much more often you complete a task. Benefits are not converted into years of life or money. Where money enters the discussion, you get a way to calculate the trade-off, not a predetermined answer. Most recommendations rely on individual studies or time-use statistics, so grades B and C predominate.

### 1. Turn “I intend to do it” into a plan specifying when, where, and what will trigger the action
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Spend 1–2 minutes per task writing an “if… then…” sentence. For example: “When I sit down at my desk at 8 a.m. on Tuesday, I’ll start by writing the introduction.” No spending.
- In plain language: Replace “I need to do this” with a concrete plan: “When I sit down at my desk at 8 a.m. on Tuesday, I’ll start by writing the introduction.” Across 94 independent tests, plans like this helped people achieve their goals, with a medium-to-large average effect. Writing the plan costs nothing and takes little effort.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis of 94 independent tests found that plans linking a specific situation to an action improved goal attainment, with a medium-to-large effect size of d = 0.65. These plans are called implementation intentions. They helped people get started, protect ongoing goal pursuit from distractions, and disengage from unsuccessful courses of action.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>
- Notes: Start with a goal you want to achieve; writing a plan cannot substitute for wanting to act. Most studies involved students or health behaviors, so take care when applying the findings to work settings.

> **Editorial clarification:** d = 0.65 is a standardized effect size, not a 65% increase in the probability of success. It summarizes different tests and does not guarantee a result for every task. A [review of how these plans work](https://cancercontrol.cancer.gov/sites/default/files/2020-06/goal_intent_attain.pdf) describes stronger effects mainly when people already had a strong intention to pursue the goal. That does not establish that weaker motivation rules out every possible benefit.

### 2. Write down your stopping conditions before you start
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: About 5 minutes at the start of a project to write, “If I haven’t reached this target by this date, I’ll stop.” No spending; following through may require you to set your pride aside.
- In plain language: Decide in advance: “If I haven’t reached the target by the agreed date, I’ll stop.” Research on if–then plans suggests that they can help people leave an unsuccessful course of action. Doing so frees up time you would otherwise keep putting into it.
- Benefit: The implementation-intention meta-analysis found that advance if–then plans also helped people disengage from failing courses of action. The potential time saving is the time they would otherwise have spent continuing along that path.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>
- Notes: Disengagement was one part of the meta-analysis, with no separate estimate of hours saved. Set the stopping conditions calmly, at the outset. If you wait, the resources you have already invested can bias the decision; see the next recommendation.

> **Editorial clarification:** the research concerns disengaging from an unsuccessful course of action, not a promise to save a specific number of hours. A stopping rule also does not establish in advance that a project is doomed. See the [meta-analysis abstract](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1).

### 3. Decide whether to continue based on future costs and benefits, not how much you have already invested
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No spending. You need to accept that time already spent cannot be recovered.
- In plain language: Resources you have already invested can make you want to continue even when doing so no longer pays off. A meta-analysis of 98 effect sizes supports this tendency for money already spent. When deciding whether to carry on, consider what you still need to put in and what you can get back. Wanting to justify past spending does not, by itself, make further effort worthwhile.
- Benefit: Sunk costs—money, effort, or time that cannot be recovered—can make people more inclined to continue an activity. A meta-analysis of 98 effect sizes confirmed this bias for monetary costs. Avoiding it can preserve the time you would otherwise spend merely because you do not want past investments to go to waste.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Arkes, H. R., & Blumer, C. (1985). The psychology of sunk cost. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 35, 124–140. <https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-5978(85)90049-4> ; Roth, S., Robbert, T., & Straus, L. (2015). On the sunk-cost effect in economic decision-making: A meta-analytic review. Business Research, 8(1), 99–138. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s40685-014-0014-8>
- Notes: For Arkes and Blumer’s theater experiment, only the qualitative finding is verified here: people who paid more for a season ticket attended more plays over the following six months. The exact attendance figures for each group still need verification. Roth and colleagues found that, when deciding whether to use something already paid for, the sunk-cost effect weakened over time and was smaller among older participants.

> **Editorial clarification:** the [Roth paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40685-014-0014-8) contains a discrepancy: its abstract reports 98 effect sizes, while its results section reports 100. The weakening over time concerns use of something already purchased. For successive investments in an ongoing project, the direction was reversed. The review selected studies of monetary costs; it does not establish the same effect size for time already spent or measure the hours saved by following this advice.

### 4. Estimate completion times from what similar tasks actually took, not just from your plan
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No spending. Take a few minutes to check how long a similar task took last time before making your estimate.
- In plain language: Students expected to finish their theses in an average of 33.9 days but actually took 55.5 days. Only 29.7% finished by their own predicted date. In another experiment, participants were asked to connect their experience of similar assignments with the current task. In that group, 60.0% finished by their predicted date, compared with 29.3% in the control group.
- Benefit: The average estimate of time remaining until thesis submission was 33.9 days, compared with an actual 55.5 days; 29.7% of students finished by their predicted date. In a separate experiment, explicitly connecting past experience with the new task brought the proportion finishing within their predicted time to 60.0%, compared with 29.3% in the control group. No significant systematic underestimation remained in the group making that connection.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366–381. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366> ; Flyvbjerg, B. (2006). From Nobel Prize to project management: Getting risks right. Project Management Journal, 37(3), 5–15. <https://doi.org/10.1177/875697280603700302> ; Halkjelsvik, T., & Jørgensen, M. (2012). From origami to software development: A review of studies on judgment-based predictions of performance time. Psychological Bulletin, 138(2), 238–271. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025996>
- Notes: Buehler’s participants were Canadian university students. This is one research paper with small samples: 37 people in the thesis study and about 40 per group in the other experiment. Flyvbjerg describes reference class forecasting—using comparable past projects—in engineering practice. Halkjelsvik’s review found underestimation more often than overestimation in engineering and management studies, but this depended on the study type and level of analysis; the size of the error varied considerably across tasks and research methods.

> **Editorial clarification:** in the [Buehler study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232558487_Exploring_the_Planning_Fallacy_Why_People_Underestimate_Their_Task_Completion_Times), 55.5 days means the time from the survey to submission, not the entire thesis project. The averages cover 33 completers out of 37 respondents. The past-experience experiment changed predictions; actual completion times and mean absolute prediction error did not significantly improve. Thus, 60.0% versus 29.3% does not mean people worked faster. Merely recalling the past was also insufficient: the effective instruction explicitly connected it with the current task.
